\chapter{State of the Art}
\label{cap:stato_arte}

This chapter reviews the methods in the literature that are related to
the components of this thesis: the segmentation networks, the automatic
annotation of the training images, the extraction of the navigation
reference from the mask, and its use by the controller. As surveyed by
Bai et al.~\cite{bai2023vision}, vision sensors provide rich appearance
and geometry cues for autonomous field guidance at low hardware cost.
Each extraction method is linked to the extractor of
Chapter~\ref{cap:geometric_reference} that is based on it or compared
with it. Dataset preparation and the implementation used in this thesis
are described in Chapters~\ref{cap:segmentation}
and~\ref{cap:geometric_reference}.

\section{Segmentation for row navigation}
\label{sec:learning_based_segmentation}

Classical pipelines combine image filtering, thresholding, and
application-specific geometric rules. Mendez et al. convert vineyard
images to grayscale, apply Gaussian filtering and Otsu thresholding,
select the largest blob, and use column pixel counts to estimate the row
center \cite[Section~3.4]{mendez2023vineyards}. Such methods can be
effective in the conditions for which their parameters were selected.
However, fixed thresholds are sensitive to changes in illumination, and
the complexity of the field and the variety of crops make it difficult
to segment the image from low-level features alone \cite{bai2023vision}.

Semantic segmentation assigns a class to each pixel, but the label
definition determines what the geometric stage can recover. De Silva
et al.\ train U-Net to predict narrow crop-row masks in sugar-beet
images. Their labels represent row structure rather than individual plant
pixels or traversable soil~\cite[Sections~3.3 and~4, Figure~5]{silva2023rowdetection}.
Navone et al.\ predict a binary vegetation mask from RGB images
using a MobileNetV3 backbone and an LR-ASPP head, then combine that
output with depth for reference extraction
\cite{navone2023ecmr,navone2025gpsfree}. Li et al.\ segment the
inter-row path instead of the plants~\cite{li2022multiroi}.

This work also segments the path between the crop rows rather than the
plants themselves. The segmentation model provides a candidate region of
drivable ground, and the geometric stage then extracts the corridor and
computes the steering reference.

\section{Semantic segmentation architectures}
\label{sec:segmentation_architectures}

\subsection{FCN, DeepLabV3, and mobile architectures}
\label{subsec:architectures_overview}

Fully Convolutional Networks (FCN) replace fully connected layers with convolutions to produce dense pixel-level predictions~\cite{long2015fcn}. DeepLabV3 incorporates atrous convolutions and atrous spatial pyramid pooling to capture multi-scale context without decreasing feature resolution~\cite{chen2017deeplab}. For embedded applications where compute resources are constrained, Howard et al.~\cite{howard2019mobilenetv3} introduced MobileNetV3, whose blocks are built on depthwise convolutions, together with the Lite Reduced Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling (LR-ASPP) segmentation head, which uses a large pooling kernel and only one $1\times1$ convolution in its pooling branch.

The choice of architecture affects both segmentation accuracy and inference speed. Deeper backbones or larger receptive fields can yield higher IoU values at the cost of additional compute. For robotic systems operating in closed loop, inference latency on edge hardware dictates the achievable control frequency.

\subsection{Transfer learning and pretraining}
\label{subsec:transfer_learning}

Transfer learning initializes network weights from models pretrained on large visual corpora such as ImageNet. The pretrained backbone is a generic feature extractor, while the task-specific segmentation head is adapted to produce class scores matching the target dataset. Reusing the layers of a pretrained network speeds up training considerably and requires significantly less training data~\cite[Chapter~11]{geron2026handson}. Long et al.\ obtain their segmentation networks by fine-tuning classifiers pretrained on ILSVRC~\cite[Section~4]{long2015fcn}. For instance, Li et al.\ train their network on maize and transfer it with small training sets to tomatoes, cucumbers, and wheat, where it reaches an MIoU of 94.01\%, 93.14\%, and 89.10\%~\cite[Abstract, Section~2.2.2]{li2022multiroi}. Navone et al.\ initialize their segmentation model with ImageNet weights and train it on synthetic AgriSeg images supplemented by 100 real crop images~\cite[Section~4.1, Table~1]{navone2025gpsfree}.

The extent of pretraining differs across implementations. Some setups retain pretrained segmentation heads and replace only the final classification layer, whereas others initialize only the backbone and train the segmentation head from scratch.

\section{Dataset preparation and model-assisted annotation}
\label{sec:dataset_annotation}

\subsection{Manual annotation challenges}
\label{subsec:manual_annotation}

Manual pixel-level annotation requires annotators to label every pixel in training images. For agricultural datasets spanning multiple crops, growth stages, and lighting conditions, this process is time-consuming and expensive. Annotation consistency depends on annotator interpretation of ambiguous boundaries, particularly where plants overlap soil or covering material.

\subsection{SAM and foundation models for segmentation}
\label{subsec:sam_annotation}

Foundation models for segmentation have been used to assist annotation. The Segment Anything project built its dataset with a model-in-the-loop data engine in three stages (assisted-manual, semi-automatic, and fully automatic), obtaining more than one billion masks on 11 million images~\cite{kirillov2023sam}. While early foundation models required spatial prompts such as bounding boxes or foreground/background clicks for every target instance, Segment Anything Model~3 (SAM~3) introduces open-vocabulary concept segmentation driven directly by textual descriptions and visual exemplars~\cite{carion2026sam3}.

In this thesis, SAM~3 is employed offline as a zero-shot concept annotator, and its masks are used without manual correction. On lettuce, networks trained on SAM~3 labels with different class sets (path, plants, plastic mulch) are compared against manual ground truth. Binary path labels generated for the complete lettuce, tomato, and cucumber collection train the network deployed on the robot. This offline annotation pipeline decouples the heavy computational burden of foundation models from the real-time inference requirements of the onboard robot controller.

\section{Extraction of the navigation reference}
\label{sec:geometric_extraction}

The geometric stage reduces a mask to a quantity the controller can use.
Depending on the representation and method, the output may be a
horizontal image position, a straight line, a crop-edge estimate, or a
curve. The methods below are grouped by the extractor of
Chapter~\ref{cap:geometric_reference} to which they are related.

\subsection{Column histograms}
\label{subsec:histogram_methods}

Histogram methods aggregate mask pixels by image column. Mendez et al.
estimate the row center from the five columns with the largest pixel
counts in the thresholded sky region
\cite[Section~3.4.3]{mendez2023vineyards}. Navone et al. use a different
input: a temporally combined vegetation mask refined with depth. SegMin
smooths its column histogram and selects the minimum. SegMinD weights
the vegetation mask by the normalized inverted depth before calculating the
histogram \cite[Section~3]{navone2025gpsfree}. In both cases the output
is a horizontal position, not a full path line. \textit{Column
histogram} (Section~\ref{subsec:c6_histogram_baseline}) is a simplified
version of SegMin that takes the column with the most path pixels in a
single frame.

\subsection{Horizontal strips and interval midpoints}
\label{subsec:strip_methods}

Li et al. combine a Faster-U-net inter-row-path mask with an adaptive
multi-ROI procedure. Horizontal regions supply the midpoints of the
path, which are used as control points for a multisegment cubic
B-spline. Their experiments use 40-pixel search regions and discuss the
trade-off between the number of points and fitting behavior
\cite[Section~2.2.3, Figure~9]{li2022multiroi}.

Replacing each interval with its midpoint before the fit discards the
distinction between the two edges of the corridor. When a disturbance
moves only one edge, such as a protruding leaf, a weed, or a shadow on
one side of the corridor, the midpoint moves by half of the displacement
of that edge. \textit{Center points}
(Section~\ref{subsec:c6_center_baseline}) follows the same scheme and
fits one straight line to the interval midpoints. \textit{Strip-IRLS}
uses the same horizontal strips but fits the two edges separately
(Section~\ref{subsec:irls_methods}).

\subsection{RANSAC line fitting}
\label{subsec:ransac_methods}

RANSAC is among the line fitting algorithms used to extract navigation
lines in agricultural vision~\cite{bai2023vision}. Wu et al.\ extract
crop-row anchor points from the segmentation masks, group them with
DBSCAN clustering, and fit navigation lines with RANSAC, with the
smallest angular and lateral errors on the central crop
row~\cite{sensors2025deeplabv3}.
RANSAC draws random samples: each iteration selects a minimal subset of
points (typically two for line fitting), computes a candidate model, and
counts inliers within a residual threshold. To achieve $99\%$ confidence
of finding an all-inlier sample for a two-point linear model when
outliers constitute $50\%$ of the data, the algorithm requires $17$
iterations, rising to $36$ iterations when the outlier ratio reaches
$65\%$, and the number of iterations grows quickly with the outlier
ratio~\cite{fischler1981ransac}. \textit{RANSAC edges}
(Section~\ref{subsec:c6_ransac_baseline}) applies RANSAC to the row
extremes of the path corridor instead of to points of the crop rows.

\subsection{Robust line fitting with IRLS}
\label{subsec:irls_methods}

Kneip et al. detect crop--ground transitions in scanlines of a stereo
elevation map, then fit a linear crop-edge model using a Huber
M-estimator and iteratively reweighted least squares. Their output is a
single cutting boundary in ground-plane coordinates along the harvesting
front, not an image-space estimate of the inter-row path
\cite[Sections~6--7]{kneip2020cropedge}.

\textit{Strip-IRLS} (Section~\ref{sec:c6_fitting}) also fits lines by
iteratively reweighted least squares, but in image space, on the two
edges of the path corridor, with a different weight function and at
most one sample per edge from each horizontal strip. Each edge is fitted
separately, so a disturbance on one edge changes only the fit of that
edge. The centerline is computed after both fits as the mean of the two
edge lines.

\section{System integration and experimental validation}
\label{sec:system_integration}

\subsection{Perception and control integration}
\label{subsec:perception_control}

Navigation systems use the extracted reference to generate motion
commands. Navone et al. convert the displacement of their histogram
reference into linear and angular velocity commands and smooth them
with an Exponential Moving Average
\cite[Section~3.4]{navone2025gpsfree}. Mendez et al. use proportional
control based on row-center displacement and the distribution of
sky pixels \cite[Section~3.5]{mendez2023vineyards}. The
controller of this thesis also filters the error with an exponential
moving average and uses a proportional steering law
(Section~\ref{subsubsec:control_node}).

\subsection{Scope of reported evaluations}
\label{subsec:evaluation_scope}

Mendez et al. report closed-loop vineyard trials, measuring centering
error from the distances to the two sides of the row
\cite[Sections~3.6 and~4]{mendez2023vineyards}. Navone et al. test in
simulation and crop fields, using LiDAR and attitude measurements to
assess displacement and heading \cite[Sections~4.3--4.4]{navone2025gpsfree}.
In the field tests of this thesis, the position of the robot is measured
with a total station (Chapter~\ref{cap:experiments}).

Segmentation IoU, image-space line error, field tracking error, and
processing time measure different parts of the system. Results from
these studies therefore provide context, not a common performance
benchmark.

\section{Positioning of this thesis}
\label{sec:thesis_positioning}

The system developed in this thesis addresses autonomous navigation in GNSS-denied crop rows using monocular RGB vision and embedded computation. Its technical positioning relative to the state of the art rests on three architectural decisions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Perceptual representation (Soil corridor versus canopy):} Rather than detecting crop stalks or foliage masks, the segmentation stage targets the navigable soil corridor. This makes the navigation reference independent of crop growth stage, canopy height, and missing plants, providing a continuous geometric guide directly at ground level.

    \item \textbf{Geometric extraction (Decoupled deterministic boundaries versus centroid reduction):} Rather than collapsing strip intervals into midpoints prior to fitting (as in Li et al.~\cite{li2022multiroi}) or fitting RANSAC lines to points of the crop rows, the proposed pipeline decouples the left and right physical corridor boundaries. Each boundary is fitted independently by iteratively reweighted least squares with ten reweighting iterations. A disturbance on one side (such as a leaf overhang or a shadow) enters only the fit of that boundary. The centerline, as the mean of the two edge lines, receives half of that boundary's deviation.

    \item \textbf{Embedded deployment feasibility:} The architecture decouples offline automatic dataset generation from onboard execution. Large foundation models (SAM~3) are restricted to offline zero-shot concept annotation, while real-time inference on the NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin is handled by a lightweight LR-ASPP network (using a MobileNetV3 backbone) paired with the deterministic IRLS geometric extractor. Without the debug video, the onboard callback takes 21.6~ms with Strip-IRLS, shorter than the 33.3~ms frame period of the 30~fps camera (Section~\ref{subsec:timing}). With Strip-IRLS, the pipeline uses no second neural network, stereo elevation mapping, or LiDAR sensor.
\end{enumerate}

The chapters that follow detail and evaluate each stage of this pipeline: Chapter~\ref{cap:architectures} formulates the deep learning architectures, Chapter~\ref{cap:segmentation} analyzes the annotation protocols and comparative model training, Chapter~\ref{cap:geometric_reference} formalizes the geometric extractors, Chapter~\ref{cap:platform} describes the Demetra robotic platform and onboard ROS integration, and Chapter~\ref{cap:experiments} reports offline benchmarks and field tests.

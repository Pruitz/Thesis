\chapter{Dataset Preparation and Comparative Model Training}
\label{cap:segmentation}
\label{cap:dataset_training}

Following the theoretical analysis of deep learning architectures in Chapter~\ref{cap:architectures}, this chapter describes the datasets, annotation pipelines, and experimental model evaluations that guide the configuration for robotic integration.

The experimental study began by fine-tuning FCN, DeepLabV3, and LR-ASPP from pretrained weights on a manually annotated lettuce dataset for an empirical comparison. Because manual segmentation was time-consuming, this initial training was limited to lettuce rather than repeated across every target crop. Its outcome led to the adoption of LR-ASPP for embedded deployment.

Following the selection of LR-ASPP, a primary experimental objective was to investigate whether an entirely automated zero-shot autolabeling pipeline using SAM~3 could generate multi-crop training datasets of sufficient quality for autonomous navigation, drastically reducing human annotation time. To evaluate this approach, SAM~3 was configured to generate three distinct label formats across the multi-crop collection (lettuce, tomato, and cucumber) without manual filtering or correction: a four-class set (background, plants, path, covering material), a three-class set (background, plants, path), and a two-class binary set (background, path). Using these autogenerated label sets, three separate multi-crop training runs were conducted with LR-ASPP to evaluate the effect of label granularity and autolabeling noise on inter-row navigation performance. Because manual ground truth was available exclusively for lettuce, the raw SAM~3 lettuce masks were directly compared against the human annotations to quantify autolabeling fidelity prior to model training.

This chapter details image acquisition, dataset partitioning, training parameters, and offline benchmark evaluations. Subsequent chapters detail geometric navigation extraction and closed-loop robot validation.

\subsection{Semantic classes and labeling targets}
\label{subsec:c5_semantic_classes}

To evaluate the impact of semantic granularity on navigation, SAM~3 was configured to generate three distinct label representations across the datasets, defining the ground truth for three corresponding training runs:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Four-class configuration (4 classes):} Segments background, plants, soil/path, and plastic mulch film (covering material). This mirrors the class taxonomy used in the manual lettuce benchmark.
\item \textbf{Three-class configuration (3 classes):} Merges non-vegetation surfaces into a single path category, segmenting background, plants, and navigable path.
\item \textbf{Binary configuration (2 classes):} Restricts segmentation strictly to background and navigable path. For robotic row-following, the path mask provides the necessary spatial boundary for centerline extraction without requiring instance discrimination or fine-grained crop classification.
\end{itemize}

\section{Dataset preparation}
\label{sec:c5_datasets}

\subsection{Lettuce dataset with manual annotation}
\label{subsec:c5_lettuce}

The manually annotated lettuce dataset, identified as \texttt{Data},
contains 762 image--mask pairs distributed across eight folders. Its
masks were prepared from the manual annotations stored in
\texttt{annotations.json}. The same manual reference is used for the
initial architecture comparison and for evaluating the models trained
with SAM~3-generated multi-crop labels. Automatically annotated lettuce
images belong to the latter training datasets, not to separate
lettuce-only training configurations.

\subsection{Automated zero-shot annotation via SAM~3}
\label{subsec:c5_sam_annotation}

Manual segmentation requires substantial human effort, motivating an investigation into whether zero-shot foundation models can automate dataset preparation entirely. To evaluate this approach, SAM~3 was employed offline in a prompt-guided regime to generate segmentation masks across all three crops: lettuce, tomato, and cucumber. Crucially, the generated masks were utilized directly without manual filtering, cleaning, or polygon correction. Retaining imperfect and noisy masks was an intentional experimental choice: the goal was to verify whether downstream model training remains resilient to real-world autolabeling errors, determining whether human intervention can be eliminated from the annotation workflow.

Because manual ground-truth annotations were available exclusively for the lettuce collection, these human masks served as the objective benchmark to evaluate the intrinsic segmentation quality and label fidelity of raw SAM~3 outputs.

For the lettuce images annotated with two foreground classes, the reported text prompts are \texttt{plants} and \texttt{central farm path}. The four-class labeling additionally uses \texttt{plastic mulch film} for covering material. Background accounts for the remaining label in each configuration. These reported lettuce prompts do not establish the exact prompts used for every crop in the multi-crop datasets. The binary configuration retains only the path annotation; crop-specific prompts and mask-composition settings for each configuration must be documented from the corresponding labeling runs.

\subsubsection{Tomato dataset}
\label{subsubsec:c5_tomato}

The tomato images were labeled using SAM~3 rather than the manual
procedure used for the initial lettuce comparison. They contribute to
the automatically annotated multi-crop training data. LR-ASPP was adopted
after the comparison of the three candidate architectures on lettuce;
no separate FCN, DeepLabV3, and LR-ASPP comparison was performed on tomato.

\subsubsection{Cucumber dataset}
\label{subsubsec:c5_cucumber}

SAM~3 was also used to label the cucumber images, which contribute to
the automatically annotated multi-crop training data. As for tomatoes,
the architecture choice followed the comparison on manually annotated
lettuce, rather than a separate comparison on cucumber.

\subsection{Dataset partitioning}
\label{subsec:c5_dataset_splits}

The reported training runs use an 80\% training, 10\% validation, and
10\% test partition with seed 42. The available \texttt{train\_model.py}
implements these proportions by assigning the integer parts of the
training and validation counts and leaving the remaining samples for
test. Applied to 762 pairs, this rule gives 609 training, 76 validation,
and 77 test samples. These counts describe the intended manual-dataset
partition; the saved indices and image lists must establish the actual
membership of each evaluated split.

The test set is not used for gradient-based parameter updates. Checkpoint
selection uses validation rather than test performance. A common seed
alone does not ensure that differently sized or ordered datasets yield
identical image partitions. Corresponding image identities must therefore
be checked when comparing the manually supervised lettuce model with the
automatically supervised multi-crop models, including whether a lettuce
evaluation image appears in an automatic model's training set.

The reported operational evaluation selects 42 images from the manual
test split belonging to \texttt{insalata\_grande1},
\texttt{insalata\_grande2}, and \texttt{insalata\_media1}. These folders
represent the row conditions selected for the robot application. The
42 images are a subset of the reported 77 test images, not an additional
independent test set. Performance on this subset describes those selected
conditions, rather than the full range of lettuce images.

\section{Architecture comparison: FCN, DeepLabV3, and LR-ASPP}
\label{sec:c5_architecture_comparison}

This comparison addresses the architecture choice before changing the
annotation procedure or crop coverage. FCN, DeepLabV3, and LR-ASPP were
trained and evaluated using the manually annotated lettuce dataset.

\subsection{Model selection and pretrained weights}
\label{subsec:c5_model_selection}

FCN, DeepLabV3, and LR-ASPP were chosen from the implementations available
in TorchVision. All three were trained on the manually annotated lettuce
dataset. Their selection was practical, rather than the outcome of a
comparison with every available architecture.

The implementation separates each network into a backbone, which extracts
image features, and a segmentation head, which produces class scores at
each output location. Transfer learning is implemented by loading
pretrained parameters and adapting the output layers to the target
labeling task, rather than initializing every network parameter from
scratch.

For FCN and DeepLabV3, the selected implementations use a ResNet-50
backbone with pretrained segmentation weights. After these weights
are loaded, the final convolution of the main classifier is replaced by
a new $1\times1$ convolution with one output channel per target class.

LR-ASPP is initialized differently. The implementation uses a
MobileNetV3-Large backbone and requests its default pretrained weights
through the \texttt{weights\_\allowbreak backbone} argument. However, the
\texttt{weights} argument of the complete segmentation model is set to
\texttt{None}. Consequently, the backbone is pretrained, whereas the
LR-ASPP segmentation head is newly initialized for the requested number
of classes.

This distinction is relevant to the interpretation of the model
comparison. The three configurations differ not only in architecture,
but also in the extent of their pretrained initialization: FCN and
DeepLabV3 retain pretrained segmentation-head layers, whereas LR-ASPP
starts with a new segmentation head.

\subsection{Training configuration and loss formulation}
\label{subsec:c5_training_config}

The optimization pipeline is designed to address class imbalance, memory constraints, and the two-stage transfer learning schedule across all evaluated architectures.

\subsubsection{Loss function and class weighting}
Agricultural scenes exhibit significant pixel imbalance between the navigable soil corridor, the plant canopy, and background areas. To prevent the optimization from biasing toward dominant categories, the models are trained using a weighted multi-class cross-entropy loss:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{WCE}}(Y^*, p) = -\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{W} w_{Y^*_{i,j}}} \sum_{i=1}^{H} \sum_{j=1}^{W} w_{Y^*_{i,j}} \log(p_{i,j, Y^*_{i,j}}),
    \label{eq:weighted_ce}
\end{equation}
where $Y^*_{i,j} \in \{0, \dots, C-1\}$ is the ground-truth class of pixel $(i,j)$, and $w_c$ is the penalty weight assigned to category $c$.

Class weights are computed exclusively on the training split $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ to avoid information leakage from validation and test partitions. For each class $c$, the total pixel count $N_c = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}} \sum_{i,j} \mathbb{I}(Y^{(k)}_{i,j} = c)$ is accumulated across all training masks. The balanced inverse frequency $\text{inv}_c$ is then normalized by the mean of all inverse frequencies:
\begin{equation}
    \text{inv}_c = \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{C-1} N_k}{C \cdot N_c}, \qquad w_c = \frac{\text{inv}_c}{\frac{1}{C}\sum_{k=0}^{C-1} \text{inv}_k}.
    \label{eq:class_weights_norm}
\end{equation}
This normalization enforces $\frac{1}{C} \sum_{c=0}^{C-1} w_c = 1.0$, which scales relative class penalties while preserving the nominal gradient magnitude during backpropagation.

For FCN and DeepLabV3, which incorporate an auxiliary classifier on the \texttt{layer3} features (output stride 8 in the dilated TorchVision backbone), the total training objective combines the primary and auxiliary losses with an auxiliary weighting factor of $0.4$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{WCE}}(\text{main}) + 0.4 \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{WCE}}(\text{aux}).
    \label{eq:total_training_loss}
\end{equation}
For LR-ASPP, which lacks an auxiliary head, training relies solely on the primary loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{WCE}}(\text{main})$.

\subsubsection{Data augmentation and two-stage optimization}
Data augmentation is applied exclusively to the training subset (\texttt{full\_\allowbreak train\_\allowbreak dataset}), incorporating random horizontal flips, scaling, and photometric adjustments to handle variable outdoor illumination. The validation and test sets (\texttt{full\_\allowbreak eval\_\allowbreak dataset}) use deterministic resizing without perturbations to preserve ground-truth geometry for objective evaluation.

Due to memory constraints imposed by high-resolution feature maps, models are trained with a batch size of 2 and \texttt{drop\_last=True} to prevent single-sample batches from destabilizing batch normalization layers.

Optimization proceeds across $200$ epochs in two distinct phases:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 (Warmup, Epochs 1--10):} Backbone parameters are frozen by setting \texttt{requires\_grad = False}. Only the newly initialized segmentation heads (and auxiliary classifiers when present) are optimized using AdamW with an initial learning rate of $10^{-3}$ and weight decay of $10^{-4}$. A cosine-annealing scheduler steps each epoch down to $\eta_{\text{min}} = 10^{-6}$. This phase prevents the randomly initialized head weights from corrupting the pretrained feature representations of the backbone.
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 (Differential Fine-Tuning, Epochs 11--200):} At epoch 10, the backbone is unfrozen. A new AdamW optimizer is instantiated with decoupled parameter groups: the backbone is assigned a reduced learning rate of $10^{-5}$ to retain general low-level visual features, while the classification heads continue at $10^{-4}$. The cosine-annealing scheduler is re-initialized across the remaining 190 epochs ($T_{\text{max}} = 190, \eta_{\text{min}} = 10^{-6}$).
\end{enumerate}

Early stopping was intentionally disabled to allow models with randomly initialized heads (such as LR-ASPP) sufficient training horizon to converge. Model checkpoints are evaluated after each epoch on the unaugmented validation set; \texttt{best.pth} is saved whenever the validation loss decreases below its historical minimum, and \texttt{last.pth} records the final epoch state.

\subsection{Selection of LR-ASPP}
\label{subsec:c5_lraspp_selection}

LR-ASPP was adopted because the substantial reduction in recorded
inference time justified accepting an mIoU approximately 1.2 percentage
points below the other two configurations. It was not the most accurate
model, but offered the practical accuracy--latency compromise sought for
robot perception. FCN and DeepLabV3 had higher IoU but substantially
longer recorded inference times. Subsequent SAM-supervised multi-crop
training therefore used LR-ASPP while varying the output label set.

This choice is based on the available offline comparison. The records
do not establish a hardware-independent speed ranking or demonstrate
that the integrated robot meets its control timing requirements.
Those requirements must be assessed using the deployed model and the
complete perception and control pipeline.

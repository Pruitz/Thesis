\chapter{Extraction of the Local Navigation Reference}
\label{cap:geometric_reference}

This chapter describes five path extractors, two of which were developed in this work. The first, \textbf{Strip-IRLS}, is the main one. It extracts the path intervals in horizontal strips of the predicted mask. Going upward from the bottom of the image, it links in each strip the interval that continues the corridor found below, and it keeps the chain that gives the most edge samples. It then fits the left and right edges of the corridor separately with iteratively reweighted least squares. \textbf{EdgeNet}, still at an early stage, is a small network that takes the same mask and regresses the two corridor edges directly. The other three are geometric baselines: \textbf{Center points}, which fits one line to the midpoints of the intervals, \textbf{Column histogram}, which takes the column with the most path pixels, and \textbf{RANSAC edges}, which fits the two edges with RANSAC on the row extremes of the corridor. All five extractors take the same input mask and return the same outputs, a straight centerline, a confidence and a status, \texttt{ok}, \texttt{degraded} or \texttt{lost}, so the controller node can run any of them. The controller does not use the centerline of a \texttt{lost} result (Chapter~\ref{cap:platform}). The extractor is chosen at start-up with a ROS parameter. All five were tested on the robot. Section~\ref{sec:c6_input} defines the input mask and the line models, Sections~\ref{sec:c6_stripirls} to~\ref{sec:c6_baseline_algorithms} describe the extractors, and Section~\ref{sec:c6_tradeoffs_and_limitations} compares their structure and lists the limitations of \textbf{Strip-IRLS}.

\section{Input mask and line models}
\label{sec:c6_input}

All five extractors take the mask $M$ predicted by the deployed binary network (Chapter~\ref{cap:segmentation}), of height $H$ and width $W$, with $x$ increasing to the right and $y$ downward. On the robot, the $640\times480$ camera image is resized to $224\times224$ pixels before the segmentation network, so $H=W=224$ (Chapter~\ref{cap:platform}). Path pixels have value 1 and all other pixels have value 0. Lines are written as functions of $y$, which avoids infinite slopes for vertical lines. All distances are in pixels of the input mask.

\textit{Strip-IRLS}, \textit{EdgeNet} and \textit{RANSAC edges} model the two corridor edges as separate straight lines, each with its own slope and intercept,
\begin{equation}
    x_L(y)=m_Ly+q_L, \qquad x_R(y)=m_Ry+q_R,
    \label{eq:c6_edges}
\end{equation}
and take the centerline as their mean,
\begin{equation}
    x_C(y)=\frac{x_L(y)+x_R(y)}{2}=m_Cy+q_C,
    \qquad m_C=\frac{m_L+m_R}{2},\quad q_C=\frac{q_L+q_R}{2}.
    \label{eq:c6_line}
\end{equation}
The edges need not be parallel, so the modeled width can decrease toward the top of the image because of perspective. The slopes and intercepts are not constrained, so the lines can follow the corridor for any position and heading of the robot inside the row. \textit{Center points} fits the centerline $x_C(y)$ directly to the midpoints of the corridor in each strip, without the edges. \textit{Column histogram} returns a vertical centerline, with $m_C=0$.

\section{Strip-IRLS}
\label{sec:c6_stripirls}

\textit{Strip-IRLS} works in five stages:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Select a lower region of interest and clean the path mask (Section~\ref{sec:c6_roi}).
    \item Extract the path intervals in horizontal strips (Section~\ref{sec:c6_candidates}).
    \item Associate intervals upward from each candidate seed and keep the best chain (Section~\ref{sec:c6_selection}).
    \item Fit the two edges separately (Section~\ref{sec:c6_fitting}).
    \item Validate the edge geometry, assign a status from a confidence score, and return the centerline (Section~\ref{sec:c6_output}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Region of interest and mask cleaning}
\label{sec:c6_roi}

Like the three baselines of Section~\ref{sec:c6_baseline_algorithms}, \textit{Strip-IRLS} uses only the lower part of the mask, the region of interest (ROI). The upper rows, where the path is not visible or cannot be distinguished, are excluded (Figure~\ref{fig:c6_roi}). The ROI starts at row $\lfloor\rho H\rfloor$ and ends at the bottom of the image. The ratio $\rho$ must be chosen based on the position of the camera and on the path, which together set the highest row where the path appears in the image. The ROI should contain the path and as few other rows as possible (Section~\ref{sec:c6_parameter_setting}). On the robot, $\rho=0.30$, chosen on the development data (Section~\ref{subsubsec:c8_parameters}), gives row 67. When needed, \textit{Strip-IRLS} extends the ROI slightly upward, so that it is filled exactly by strips of equal height (Section~\ref{sec:c6_candidates}). On the robot, the ROI is extended from row 67 to row 64.

The path pixels inside the ROI are cleaned before the intervals are extracted. An opening with a $5\times5$ elliptical kernel removes thin protrusions and small isolated regions, then a closing with a $9\times9$ rectangular kernel fills small holes and gaps. The three baselines apply the same opening and closing. Finally, the connected components with fewer than 500 pixels are removed. In Figure~\ref{fig:c6_roi}, the dark gray regions inside the ROI are the pixels removed by the cleaning. Only the remaining path pixels, the cleaned mask $\widetilde M$, are passed to the interval extraction.

\subsection{Horizontal strips and intervals}
\label{sec:c6_candidates}

The ROI is divided into $N$ strips of equal height, indexed from the bottom. $N$ is a parameter of the extractor, set to 16 on the robot. The strip height is $h=\lceil\,(H-\lfloor\rho H\rfloor)/N\,\rceil$, which is the smallest height for which $N$ strips cover the rows from $\lfloor\rho H\rfloor$ to the bottom of the image. When the number of these rows is not a multiple of $N$, the $N$ strips cover a few more rows, so the ROI is extended upward and starts at $y_0=H-Nh$. On the robot, the 157 rows from 67 to 223 are not a multiple of 16, so $h=10$~px and the ROI starts at row 64. The extension depends only on $H$, $\rho$ and $N$, so the ROI is the same for every frame. Strip $s$ covers the image rows from $H-(s+1)h$ to $H-sh-1$, and its center is at row $H-(s+0.5)h$. On the robot, strip 0 covers rows 214 to 223.

In each strip, a column is active if it contains at least one path pixel of $\widetilde M$. Runs of active columns separated by at most 40 inactive columns are merged into one interval $[\ell_i,r_i]$ of width $d_i=r_i-\ell_i+1$, so that small holes and shadows inside the corridor do not split it.

An endpoint within 2~px of the image border is flagged as clipped. There the corridor leaves the image, so the endpoint is the image border and not the edge of the corridor. Two kinds of intervals are discarded: those narrower than 8~px, and those clipped on both sides when path pixels cover less than 70\% of the strip.

\subsection{Bottom-up association and chain selection}
\label{sec:c6_selection}

The association decides which intervals belong to the corridor. The endpoints of these intervals are the samples of the two edge fits (Section~\ref{sec:c6_fitting}). It works in two steps: one or more chains of intervals are grown upward from the bottom of the ROI, then one chain is selected.

\textbf{Growing the chains.} A chain starts from a seed, an interval of the bottom six strips, where the corridor is closest to the robot. On the robot, these six strips cover 60 image rows. From the seed, the chain is grown upward, one strip at a time. In each strip, each interval is compared with the last accepted interval $[\ell_p,r_p]$, of width $d_p$, through the boundary displacement $\delta_i=\max(|\ell_i-\ell_p|,|r_i-r_p|)$, which is the larger shift of its two endpoints. An interval is a candidate only if this shift is within the jump limit $\Delta_{\max}$. The limit is 28~px, or half the width of the last accepted interval when this is wider than 56~px. Among the candidates, the accepted interval is the one with the lowest cost $J_i$, which adds to the shift a penalty on the change of width:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\max}=\max(28,\,0.5\,d_p),\qquad J_i=\delta_i+0.4\,|d_i-d_p|.
    \label{eq:c6_cost4}
\end{equation}
The accepted interval becomes the reference for the next strip. The choice is greedy: an accepted interval is never revised later. A strip without candidates is skipped, and the reference does not change. The chain ends at the top of the ROI or at the fourth consecutive skipped strip, so on the robot it can cross a gap of up to 30 rows. No information is carried from one frame to the next.

The seeds are tried from strip 0 upward, and a chain is grown from each seed that does not already belong to an earlier chain. A chain grown from an interval of an earlier chain would start from the same reference and accept the same intervals, so it would only repeat the upper part of that chain. When the first chain contains all the intervals of the bottom six strips, there is only one chain. Other chains are grown when some of these intervals are not in the first chain, for example the intervals of a blob or of an adjacent corridor next to the path.

\textbf{Selecting a chain.} The complete chains are compared. The selected chain is the one that gives the most samples to the edge fits. The samples are the endpoints not clipped at the image border. An interval inside the image gives two samples, an interval clipped on one side gives one, and an interval clipped on both sides gives none. A chain along the image border therefore has fewer samples than a chain of the same length inside the corridor. Among chains with the same number of samples, the one with the lowest sum of the costs $J_i$ is selected. If the bottom six strips contain no interval, or the selected chain has fewer than three intervals, the result is \texttt{lost}.

Figure~\ref{fig:c6_chain} shows the association on the mask of Figure~\ref{fig:geometric_strips_projection}. The blob at the bottom left has more than 500 pixels, so the cleaning does not remove it, and it gives one interval in each of strips 0 to 4. Strip 0 contains two intervals, and the one on the left, in the blob, is tried first as a seed. Its chain accepts the blob intervals up to strip 4. The only interval of strip 5 belongs to the corridor, and it is shifted by 113~px from the interval accepted in strip 4, well beyond the jump limit of 28~px. The same happens in the next strips, so the chain finds no other candidate and ends at strip 4. The corridor interval of strip 0 is not in this chain, so a second chain is grown from it. This chain accepts one interval in each strip up to strip 13. Strips 14 and 15 contain no interval, and the chain ends at the top of the ROI. In the bottom six strips, no interval is left outside the two chains, so no other seed is available and no other chain is grown (Figure~\ref{fig:c6_chain}a). The corridor chain has 28 samples and the blob chain has 10, so the corridor chain is selected, although its total cost is higher, 122 against 26.8 (Figure~\ref{fig:c6_chain}b). \subsection{Separate fitting of the corridor edges}
\label{sec:c6_fitting}

The two edges are fitted separately. The left edge uses the left endpoints $\ell_i$ of the accepted intervals, and the right edge uses the right endpoints $r_i$. Clipped endpoints are not used, so an interval clipped on one side gives a sample only to the other edge, and the two edges can have samples in different strips. Each edge needs at least two samples. If one edge has fewer, the result is \texttt{lost}, because the centerline is never estimated from a single edge.

Each edge is fitted with iteratively reweighted least squares. The first fit uses uniform weights, and ten reweighting iterations follow. At each iteration, the distance of each sample from the current line gives its new weight, and the line is fitted again. A sample far from the line gets a low weight. How fast the weight decreases is set by the residual scale $\sigma$, a parameter of the extractor. A sample at distance $\sigma$ from the line gets weight 0.5. On the robot, $\sigma=18$~px (Section~\ref{sec:c6_parameter_setting}). At iteration $t$, the residuals and the new weights are
\begin{equation}
    e_i^{(t)}=x_i-\bigl(m^{(t)}y_i+q^{(t)}\bigr),\qquad
    w_i^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{1+(e_i^{(t)}/\sigma)^2},
    \label{eq:c6_reweight}
\end{equation}
and the line is fitted again by weighted least squares, with each weight applied to the residual before it is squared:
\begin{equation}
    (m,q)=\mathop{\arg\min}_{m,q}
        \sum_i \left[w_i\bigl(x_i-(my_i+q)\bigr)\right]^2.
    \label{eq:c6_objective}
\end{equation}
Each squared residual is therefore weighted by $w_i^2$. The weights are not accumulated across iterations, since they are computed again from the current line only. The reweighting reduces the effect of isolated outliers, but not of many samples from outside the corridor, because they lie on a consistent line and the fit follows them. Figure~\ref{fig:robust_edge_fitting} shows the reweighting on the samples of the selected chain of Figure~\ref{fig:c6_chain}b.

The two edges of a corridor converge upward in the image, but they cannot meet in the rows where the chain has samples, because the corridor has a width there. If the fitted edges meet in these rows, the intervals above the meeting point do not follow the corridor. They can come, for example, from a region attached to the corridor, and they bend the fits. Before the final fit, the edges are therefore fitted with the same reweighting and checked. With $\Delta m=m_R-m_L$ and $\Delta q=q_R-q_L$, the modeled width is $D(y)=x_R(y)-x_L(y)=\Delta m\,y+\Delta q$, and the edges converge upward when $\Delta m>0$. The width falls to the minimum interval width $d_{\min}=8$~px at the row
\begin{equation}
    y_\times=\frac{d_{\min}-\Delta q}{\Delta m}.
    \label{eq:c6_crossing}
\end{equation}
If the edges converge upward and $y_\times$ is below the strip center of the highest accepted interval, the intervals whose strip center is above $y_\times$ are discarded, together with their samples on both edges, and both edges are fitted again. The check is made at most three times, and it stops earlier in three cases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item There is no crossing: the edges do not converge upward, or $y_\times$ is not below the highest accepted interval.
    \item The discard would leave fewer than three intervals. The intervals are then kept, and the check stops with the crossing still present.
    \item An edge has fewer than two samples, and the result is \texttt{lost}. The previous rule counts only the intervals, so this can happen after a discard, when the remaining intervals are clipped on that side.
\end{itemize}
Otherwise, the final fit uses the remaining intervals. A crossing can therefore still be present in three cases: when too few intervals would remain, after three passes, or when the edges converge downward, which the check does not treat. It is handled by the width check of Section~\ref{sec:c6_output}.

\subsection{Validation, confidence, and output}
\label{sec:c6_output}

After the final fit, the extractor checks in which rows the two edges are at least $d_{\min}$ apart. Above the samples the edges can be closer than $d_{\min}$, but not in the rows where the chain has samples: every accepted interval is at least $d_{\min}$ wide (Section~\ref{sec:c6_candidates}), and edges closer than $d_{\min}$ would not describe the intervals they were fitted on. This can still happen in the three cases listed at the end of Section~\ref{sec:c6_fitting}. The check covers the rows from the highest sample that remains after the crossing check (that is the final fit), among the samples of both edges, down to the bottom of the image, since the centerline is drawn down to the bottom. The rows where the edges are at least $d_{\min}$ apart form the valid range. Since the width $D(y)$ changes linearly with the row, there is only one row where the width is exactly $d_{\min}$. This row is $y_\times$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:c6_crossing}, now computed on the final edges. On one side of $y_\times$ the edges are far enough apart, and on the other side they are too close. If the edges converge upward, the rows above $y_\times$ are excluded. If they converge downward, the rows below $y_\times$ are excluded. If they are parallel, the width is the same in every row, so the valid range contains all the rows or none. The top row of the valid range, $y_v^{\mathrm{top}}$, is used by the tracking score $T$ below.

The width check fails, and the result is \texttt{lost}, in two cases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $y_\times$ lies between the lowest sample and a row $0.05H$ (11~px) below the highest sample. The edges then cross in rows where the chain has samples, which indicates a chain that joins different regions or a wrong fit. A crossing outside these rows can only shorten the valid range.
    \item The valid range is shorter than 10\% of the ROI height $H-y_0$, that is 16 of 160 rows on the robot. The corridor is then seen in too few rows.
\end{itemize}

The confidence $C$ combines four scores between 0 and 1. They use the seed and the accepted intervals of the selected chain that were not discarded by the crossing check of Section~\ref{sec:c6_fitting}. Below they are called the intervals of the chain.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tracking $T$.} The fraction of the strips of the valid range that contain an interval of the chain. It is the number of intervals of the chain divided by the number of strips whose center lies in the valid range. It is limited to 1 because intervals of the chain above the valid range are also counted. $T$ is 1 when every strip of the valid range has an interval of the chain, and it is low when the chain skips many of them. For example, a chain from strip 0 to strip 13 that skips two strips has 12 intervals. If the crossing check discards the intervals of strips 12 and 13, 10 intervals remain and the valid range starts at the center of strip 11. Then $T=10/12\approx0.83$: the two skipped strips lower $T$, while the two discarded intervals lie above the valid range and do not.
    \item \textbf{Fit $F$.} How close the samples are to the fitted edge lines. With $\bar e$ the mean horizontal distance of the samples of both edges from their lines, $F=\operatorname{clip}(1-\bar e/\sigma,0,1)$. $F$ is 1 when the samples lie on the lines and falls to 0 when $\bar e$ reaches $\sigma=18$~px. It is low when the edges of the mask are irregular or when some samples come from outside the corridor. The distances are not weighted, so a sample with a low weight in the fit still counts fully in $\bar e$.
    \item \textbf{Width regularity $A$.} How regularly the width of the corridor changes from one interval to the next. With straight edges, the width changes linearly, so its second differences are close to zero. $A$ uses the widths of the intervals of the chain that are not clipped on either side, since the width of a clipped interval is not the corridor width. The 90th percentile of the absolute second differences, divided by the median width, gives $E_D$, and $A=\operatorname{clip}(1-E_D/0.30,0,1)$. $A$ is 1 when the width changes linearly and falls to 0 when $E_D$ reaches 0.30. It is low when the width jumps, for example when some intervals are widened by a region attached to the corridor. With fewer than three unclipped intervals, $A$ is 1.
    \item \textbf{Gaps $G$.} The longest hole in the chain. With $g_{\max}$ the longest run of strips skipped between two intervals of the chain, $G=\operatorname{clip}(1-g_{\max}/3,0,1)$. $G$ is 1 when the chain skips no strip and 0 when it skips three strips in a row, the longest gap the chain can cross. $T$ counts all the skipped strips, while $G$ depends only on the longest run, so one long gap lowers $G$ more than several short ones.
\end{itemize}
The confidence is their weighted sum:
\begin{equation}
    C=0.3125\,T+0.3125\,F+0.25\,A+0.125\,G.
    \label{eq:c6_confidence4}
\end{equation}
The result is \texttt{lost} if the width check fails, if $\bar e>2\sigma$, or if $C<0.38$. Otherwise it is \texttt{degraded} for $C<0.60$ and \texttt{ok} for $C\geq0.60$. $C$ is a heuristic score, not a calibrated probability.

For a \texttt{lost} result, no centerline model is returned. Otherwise, the centerline model $x_C(y)$ is returned and the centerline is drawn in the valid range. The controller reads the lateral error from the model, not from the drawn mask (Chapter~\ref{cap:platform}).

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ROI start $\rho$.} The ROI starts at row $\lfloor\rho H\rfloor$, and the rows above it are not used (Section~\ref{sec:c6_roi}). It should start at the highest row where the path appears, which depends on the position of the camera and on the path. Rows above the path can add regions that are not path. Their intervals can enter the chain and pull the edge fits away from the corridor. Because the ROI is a whole number of strips, its start moves in steps of $N$ rows when $\rho$ changes. On the robot, $\rho=0.30$, chosen on the development masks (Section~\ref{subsubsec:c8_parameters}).
    \item \textbf{Number of strips $N$.} The ROI is divided into $N$ strips of equal height (Section~\ref{sec:c6_candidates}). Each strip gives at most one interval to the chain, so more strips give more samples to the edge fits. The strips are then thinner, and a hole in the mask splits or empties a thin strip more easily, because a column is active if any row of the strip contains path. $N$ should therefore be the largest value for which the holes of the predicted mask do not split or empty the strips. On the robot, $N=16$, and each strip is 10 rows high.
    \item \textbf{Merging gap.} Two runs of active columns in the same strip separated by at most this number of columns are merged into one interval (Section~\ref{sec:c6_candidates}). It should be larger than the gaps inside the corridor, such as holes and shadows, so that they do not split it. It should be smaller than the crop row between two adjacent corridors, so that the two corridors are not merged into one interval. On the robot it is 40~px, because the field tests have no corridors side by side.
    \item \textbf{Minimum interval width, minimum area and jump limit $\Delta_{\max}$.} The cleaning removes the connected components with fewer pixels than the minimum area (Section~\ref{sec:c6_roi}), and the intervals narrower than the minimum interval width $d_{\min}$ are discarded (Section~\ref{sec:c6_candidates}). The same $d_{\min}$ is the smallest corridor width accepted by the crossing check and by the validation (Sections~\ref{sec:c6_fitting} and~\ref{sec:c6_output}). Both values should be below the smallest corridor region and the narrowest corridor in the mask, so that the corridor itself is never removed. On the robot they are 500 pixels and 8~px. The jump limit is the largest shift of the endpoints from one accepted interval to the next (Section~\ref{sec:c6_selection}). It should be above the lateral shift of the corridor between two strips, so that the chain follows the corridor, \textcolor{red}{which grows with the offset of the robot and with the pitch and roll of the camera}, and below the shift to the regions that must be rejected. On the robot it is $\max(28,\,0.5\,d_p)$~px.
    \item \textbf{Residual scale $\sigma$ and thresholds.} The residual scale sets how fast the weight of a sample decreases with its distance from the edge line (Eq.~\eqref{eq:c6_reweight}). It also sets the fit score $F$ and the rejection of the result when the mean residual is above $2\sigma$ (Section~\ref{sec:c6_output}). It should be close to the irregularity of the edges of the predicted mask. A smaller $\sigma$ also lowers the weight of correct samples on an irregular edge, and a larger $\sigma$ gives a high weight to the outliers too. On the robot, $\sigma=18$~px. The thresholds on the confidence $C$ assign the status: below 0.38 the result is \texttt{lost}, and from 0.60 it is \texttt{ok}. They are not calibrated.
\end{itemize}

\section{EdgeNet, an edge regression network}
\label{sec:c6_network}

\textit{EdgeNet} is a first test of replacing the geometric steps of \textit{Strip-IRLS} with a learned model. Like the second network of M{\"u}ller et al.~\cite{muller2018driving} (Section~\ref{subsec:bilateral_vs_centroids}), it takes a segmentation mask and not the image, but it regresses the two corridor edges instead of waypoints.

The network is a small CNN of about 0.8 million parameters, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:c6_edgenet}. Its input is the $224\times224$ mask, and it has five outputs: the $x$ positions of the left and right edges at rows 156 and 223, and the logit of the probability $p_{\mathrm{valid}}$ that the mask contains a corridor. Row 156 is the row read by the controller and row 223 is the last row of the image. Each edge line is the line through its predicted points at rows 156 and 223, and the centerline is the mean of the two lines, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:c6_line}.

    \centering
    \input{images/architectures/edgenet_tikz}%
    \caption{Architecture of \textit{EdgeNet}. Each box is a feature map, colored by the layer that produces it and labeled with channels $\times$ height $\times$ width. The two maps of a block have the same size and share one label. The thickness of a box grows with the channels and its height with the size of the map, not to scale. The mask is averaged over $2\times2$ blocks, and two channels with the $x$ and $y$ coordinates of each pixel, scaled to $[-1,1]$, are added, so the convolutions also see where each pixel is. The confidence is $p_{\mathrm{valid}}$ multiplied by the intersection over union, from row 64 to the bottom, between the region enclosed by the two edge lines and the path pixels of the mask. The status is \texttt{lost} if $p_{\mathrm{valid}}<0.5$, if the modeled width at row 223 is below 8~px, or if the confidence is below 0.38, and \texttt{degraded} if it is below 0.60. The thresholds 0.38 and 0.60 are those of \textit{Strip-IRLS} and are not calibrated for this confidence. The width is checked at row 223 and not at row 156 because of the labels of the training masks, described below.

The network is trained only on synthetic masks, with labels computed from the corridor model and not from an extractor, so that it does not learn the errors of an algorithm. M{\"u}ller et al.\ obtain realistic errors by applying a real segmentation network to simulated images. Here the errors of the segmentation are modeled by hand in the generator. The generator draws perspective corridors whose apex, the row where the two edges meet, goes from above the image down to row 190. A quarter of the corridors are curved. The generator adds disturbances found in predicted masks, such as irregular edges, adjacent corridors, holes and blobs. Above the apex the labels extend the edge lines, so when the apex is below row 156 the labeled edges are swapped at that row. For this reason the width is checked at row 223. About 14\% of the 300\,000 training masks have no valid corridor. The generator does not use the synthetic test sets of Chapter~\ref{cap:experiments}, but it was written by the same author, who knew their scenarios. The loss is a smooth L1 loss on the four edge positions, plus twice the same loss on the centerline at row 156, both only on masks with a valid corridor, plus 0.2 times the binary cross-entropy on the validity. On the 17\,266 masks with a valid corridor among 20\,000 synthetic validation masks, the median centerline error at row 156 is 1.7~px and the 90th percentile 13.0~px. These masks were also used to choose the checkpoint with the lowest 90th percentile. These values say nothing about real masks. The network is exported to ONNX and run on the robot with ONNX Runtime.

\section{Baseline extractors}
\label{sec:c6_baseline_algorithms}

\textit{Strip-IRLS} and \textit{EdgeNet} are compared with the three geometric baselines of this section.

\subsection{Center points}
\label{subsec:c6_center_baseline}

The \textit{Center points} extractor uses the same mask cleaning and interval extraction as \textit{Strip-IRLS}, with a merging gap of 30 columns. Its ROI starts at $y_0=\lfloor 0.30H\rfloor=67$ and is not extended. Its $N=24$ strips are $\lfloor (H-y_0)/N\rfloor=6$~px high, so they cover rows 80 to 223, and rows 67 to 79 belong to no strip. Each interval is reduced to its midpoint $c=(\ell+r)/2$ and its width $d=r-\ell+1$. The seed is the interval of the bottom six strips with the most path pixels, with ties broken by width. Intervals are associated upward with the cost
\begin{equation}
    J_i = |c_i - c_p| + 0.4\, |d_i - d_p|,
    \label{eq:c6_center_cost}
\end{equation}
with $c_p$ and $d_p$ the midpoint and width of the last accepted interval, under the jump limit $|c_i - c_p| \le \max(28, 0.5\, d_p)$, with the same stopping rule and the same minimum of three intervals as \textit{Strip-IRLS}.

One line is fitted to the midpoints with four iterations of the weights of Eq.~\eqref{eq:c6_reweight}. Unlike \textit{Strip-IRLS}, the initial weights are proportional to the pixel count of each interval (normalized to mean one), the weights multiply across iterations, and clipped intervals are not excluded. The confidence combines the same four scores as \textit{Strip-IRLS} (Section~\ref{sec:c6_output}), tracking $T$, fit $F$, regularity of the width $A$ and gaps $G$, with weights 0.30, 0.35, 0.25 and 0.10. $T$ is the number of accepted intervals divided by the number of all the strips, so the strips above the end of the corridor also lower it. $A$ uses the widths of all the accepted intervals, and $G$ also counts the skipped strips that end the association. The result is \texttt{lost} if the mean residual exceeds $2\sigma$ or if the confidence is below 0.38, and \texttt{degraded} if it is below 0.60. Because the midpoints are formed before the fit, a disturbance on one edge moves the midpoint and enters the centerline.

\subsection{Column histogram}
\label{subsec:c6_histogram_baseline}

The \textit{Column histogram} extractor is a simplified version of the column projection of SegMin. SegMin sums a plant mask, accumulated over recent frames and refined with depth, along the image columns, smooths the profile, and takes its minimum~\cite{navone2025gpsfree}. \textit{Column histogram} uses the path mask of a single frame, without depth or smoothing, and takes the maximum. Its ROI starts at $y_0=\lfloor 0.30H\rfloor=67$, and the mask is cleaned with the same opening and closing as \textit{Strip-IRLS}. In each column, the extractor counts the path pixels of the cleaned ROI, and the target column $x_{\text{target}}$ is the column with the most path pixels. If several columns have the maximum count, as when the corridor fills the whole height of the ROI, $x_{\text{target}}$ is the center of the widest group of adjacent ones. The confidence is the maximum count divided by the ROI height $H-y_0$, with the same thresholds as \textit{Strip-IRLS} (0.38 and 0.60). The output is the vertical line $x=x_{\text{target}}$, so the method gives a lateral position but no heading.

\subsection{RANSAC edges}
\label{subsec:c6_ransac_baseline}

RANSAC line fitting is used in agricultural row detection on points of the crop rows~\cite{bai2023vision,sensors2025deeplabv3}. The RANSAC extractor applies it to the edges of the segmented corridor, without strips or bottom-up association.

The ROI starts at $y_0=\lfloor 0.30H\rfloor=67$ and the mask is cleaned with the same opening and closing as \textit{Strip-IRLS}. The extractor then keeps one connected component, a large one that starts low in the image. In each ROI row of this component, its leftmost and rightmost pixels are edge points. As in \textit{Strip-IRLS}, the endpoints at the image border are not used.

Each edge is fitted with RANSAC~\cite{fischler1981ransac}: in 100 trials, two random points define a line, and the points within 18~px of it are inliers. The line with the most inliers is refitted by least squares on its inliers, and the centerline is the mean of the two edge lines, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:c6_line}. The random draws use a fixed seed, so the same mask always gives the same line.

The result is \texttt{lost} if one edge has too few points or covers too few rows, for example when the mask is a band of path across the bottom of the image at the end of a row. Otherwise a score combines the fraction of inliers, their residual, the vertical extent of the component and the regularity of its width. Below 0.38 the result is \texttt{lost}, and from 0.60 it is \texttt{ok}.

\textit{Strip-IRLS} has four structural limitations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Seed dependence.} If the bottom six strips contain no interval, for example because the mask in front of the robot is missing, the frame is rejected even if the upper part of the corridor is segmented. Within a chain the association is greedy, so at a fork the chain follows the branch with the lowest cost in the next strip, even if that branch ends soon after.
    \item \textbf{Thresholds in pixels.} The jump limit, the merging gap, the minimum interval width, the minimum component area and $\sigma$ are in pixels of the $224\times224$ mask (Section~\ref{sec:c6_parameter_setting}). \textcolor{red}{Camera pitch and roll on uneven ground move the corridor in the image, and a displacement above the jump limit stops the association early.}
    \item \textbf{Straight edges.} Both edges are straight lines, so curved rows and headland turns are not represented. Li et al.~\cite{li2022multiroi} use a cubic B-spline for curved paths.
    \item \textbf{One-sided gaps.} Each accepted interval gives one sample to each edge, unless the endpoint on that side is clipped. Where plants are missing on one side, the interval can widen or merge with a nearby region, and if the displacement exceeds the jump limit for more than three consecutive strips, the association stops. If the missing side extends the interval to the image border, the endpoint on that side is clipped, and the frame is rejected when an edge has fewer than two non-clipped samples.
\end{enumerate}

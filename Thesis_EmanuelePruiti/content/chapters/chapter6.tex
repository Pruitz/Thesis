\chapter{Extraction of the Local Navigation Reference}
\label{cap:geometric_reference}

This chapter describes five path extractors, two of which were developed in this work. The first, \textbf{Strip-IRLS}, is the main one. It extracts the path intervals in horizontal strips of the predicted mask. Going upward from the bottom of the image, it links in each strip the interval that continues the corridor found below, and it keeps the chain that gives the most edge samples. It then fits the left and right edges of the corridor separately with iteratively reweighted least squares. \textbf{EdgeNet}, still at an early stage, is a small network that takes the same mask and regresses the two corridor edges directly. The other three are geometric baselines: \textbf{Center points}, which fits one line to the midpoints of the intervals, \textbf{Column histogram}, which takes the column with the most path pixels, and \textbf{RANSAC edges}, which fits the two edges with RANSAC on the row extremes of the corridor. All five extractors take the same input mask and return the same outputs, a straight centerline, a confidence and a status, \texttt{ok}, \texttt{degraded} or \texttt{lost}, so the controller node can run any of them. The controller does not use the centerline of a \texttt{lost} result (Chapter~\ref{cap:platform}). The extractor is chosen at start-up with a ROS parameter. All five were tested on the robot. Section~\ref{sec:c6_input} defines the input mask and the line models, Sections~\ref{sec:c6_stripirls} to~\ref{sec:c6_baseline_algorithms} describe the extractors, and Section~\ref{sec:c6_tradeoffs_and_limitations} compares their structure and lists the limitations of \textbf{Strip-IRLS}.

\section{Input mask and line models}
\label{sec:c6_input}

All five extractors take the mask $M$ predicted by the deployed binary network (Chapter~\ref{cap:segmentation}), of height $H$ and width $W$, with $x$ increasing to the right and $y$ downward. On the robot, the $640\times480$ camera image is resized to $224\times224$ pixels before the segmentation network, so $H=W=224$ (Chapter~\ref{cap:platform}). Path pixels have value 1 and all other pixels have value 0. Lines are written as functions of $y$, which avoids infinite slopes for vertical lines. All distances are in pixels of the input mask.

\textit{Strip-IRLS}, \textit{EdgeNet} and \textit{RANSAC edges} model the two corridor edges as separate straight lines, each with its own slope and intercept,
\begin{equation}
    x_L(y)=m_Ly+q_L, \qquad x_R(y)=m_Ry+q_R,
    \label{eq:c6_edges}
\end{equation}
and take the centerline as their mean,
\begin{equation}
    x_C(y)=\frac{x_L(y)+x_R(y)}{2}=m_Cy+q_C,
    \qquad m_C=\frac{m_L+m_R}{2},\quad q_C=\frac{q_L+q_R}{2}.
    \label{eq:c6_line}
\end{equation}
The edges need not be parallel, so the modeled width can decrease toward the top of the image because of perspective. The slopes and intercepts are not constrained, so the lines can follow the corridor for any position and heading of the robot inside the row. \textit{Strip-IRLS} then rejects the edges that do not converge upward (Section~\ref{sec:c6_output}). \textit{Center points} fits the centerline $x_C(y)$ directly to the midpoints of the corridor in each strip, without the edges. \textit{Column histogram} returns a vertical centerline, with $m_C=0$.

\section{Strip-IRLS}
\label{sec:c6_stripirls}

\textit{Strip-IRLS} works in five stages:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Select a lower region of interest and clean the path mask (Section~\ref{sec:c6_roi}).
    \item Extract the path intervals in horizontal strips (Section~\ref{sec:c6_candidates}).
    \item Associate intervals upward from each candidate seed and keep the best chain (Section~\ref{sec:c6_selection}).
    \item Fit the two edges separately (Section~\ref{sec:c6_fitting}).
    \item Validate the edge geometry, assign a status from a confidence score, and return the centerline (Section~\ref{sec:c6_output}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Region of interest and mask cleaning}
\label{sec:c6_roi}

Like the three baselines of Section~\ref{sec:c6_baseline_algorithms}, \textit{Strip-IRLS} uses only the lower part of the mask, the region of interest (ROI). The upper rows, where the path is not visible or cannot be distinguished, are excluded (Figure~\ref{fig:c6_roi}). The ROI starts at row $\lfloor\rho H\rfloor$ and ends at the bottom of the image. The ratio $\rho$ must be chosen based on the position of the camera and on the path, which together set the highest row where the path appears in the image. The ROI should contain the path and as few other rows as possible (Section~\ref{sec:c6_parameter_setting}). On the robot, $\rho=0.30$, chosen on the development data (Section~\ref{subsubsec:c8_parameters}), gives row 67. When needed, \textit{Strip-IRLS} extends the ROI slightly upward, so that it is filled exactly by strips of equal height (Section~\ref{sec:c6_candidates}). On the robot, the ROI is extended from row 67 to row 64.

The path pixels inside the ROI are cleaned before the intervals are extracted. An opening with a $5\times5$ elliptical kernel removes thin protrusions and small isolated regions, then a closing with a $9\times9$ rectangular kernel fills small holes and gaps. The three baselines apply the same opening and closing. Finally, the connected components with fewer than 500 pixels are removed. In Figure~\ref{fig:c6_roi}, the dark gray regions inside the ROI are the pixels removed by the cleaning. Only the remaining path pixels, the cleaned mask $\widetilde M$, are passed to the interval extraction.

\subsection{Horizontal strips and intervals}
\label{sec:c6_candidates}

The ROI is divided into $N$ strips of equal height, indexed from the bottom. $N$ is a parameter of the extractor, set to 16 on the robot. The strip height is $h=\lceil\,(H-\lfloor\rho H\rfloor)/N\,\rceil$, which is the smallest height for which $N$ strips cover the rows from $\lfloor\rho H\rfloor$ to the bottom of the image. When the number of these rows is not a multiple of $N$, the $N$ strips cover a few more rows, so the ROI is extended upward and starts at $y_0=H-Nh$. On the robot, the 157 rows from 67 to 223 are not a multiple of 16, so $h=10$~px and the ROI starts at row 64. The extension depends only on $H$, $\rho$ and $N$, so the ROI is the same for every frame. Strip $s$ covers the image rows from $H-(s+1)h$ to $H-sh-1$, and its center is at row $H-(s+0.5)h$. On the robot, strip 0 covers rows 214 to 223.

In each strip, a column is active if it contains at least one path pixel of $\widetilde M$. Runs of active columns separated by at most 30 inactive columns are merged into one interval $[\ell_i,r_i]$ of width $d_i=r_i-\ell_i+1$, so that small holes and shadows inside the corridor do not split it.

An endpoint within 2~px of the image border is flagged as clipped. There the corridor leaves the image, so the endpoint is the image border and not the edge of the corridor. Two kinds of intervals are discarded: those narrower than 8~px, and those clipped on both sides when path pixels cover less than 70\% of the strip.

\subsection{Bottom-up association and chain selection}
\label{sec:c6_selection}

The association decides which intervals belong to the corridor. The endpoints of these intervals are the samples of the two edge fits (Section~\ref{sec:c6_fitting}). It works in two steps: one or more chains of intervals are grown upward from the bottom of the ROI, then one chain is selected.

\textbf{Growing the chains.} A chain starts from a seed, an interval of the bottom six strips, where the corridor is closest to the robot. On the robot, these six strips cover 60 image rows. From the seed, the chain is grown upward, one strip at a time. In each strip, each interval is compared with the last accepted interval $[\ell_p,r_p]$, of width $d_p$, through the boundary displacement $\delta_i=\max(|\ell_i-\ell_p|,|r_i-r_p|)$, which is the larger shift of its two endpoints. An interval is a candidate only if this shift is within the jump limit $\Delta_{\max}$. The limit is 28~px, or half the width of the last accepted interval when this is wider than 56~px. Among the candidates, the accepted interval is the one with the lowest cost $J_i$, which adds to the shift a penalty on the change of width:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\max}=\max(28,\,0.5\,d_p),\qquad J_i=\delta_i+0.4\,|d_i-d_p|.
    \label{eq:c6_cost4}
\end{equation}
The accepted interval becomes the reference for the next strip. The choice is greedy: an accepted interval is never revised later. A strip without candidates is skipped, and the reference does not change. The chain ends at the top of the ROI or at the fourth consecutive skipped strip, so on the robot it can cross a gap of up to 30 rows. No information is carried from one frame to the next.

The seeds are tried from strip 0 upward, and a chain is grown from each seed that does not already belong to an earlier chain. A chain grown from an interval of an earlier chain would start from the same reference and accept the same intervals, so it would only repeat the upper part of that chain. When the first chain contains all the intervals of the bottom six strips, there is only one chain. Other chains are grown when some of these intervals are not in the first chain, for example the intervals of a blob or of an adjacent corridor next to the path.

\textbf{Selecting a chain.} The complete chains are compared. The selected chain is the one that gives the most samples to the edge fits. The samples are the endpoints not clipped at the image border. An interval inside the image gives two samples, an interval clipped on one side gives one, and an interval clipped on both sides gives none. A chain along the image border therefore has fewer samples than a chain of the same length inside the corridor. Among chains with the same number of samples, the one with the lowest sum of the costs $J_i$ is selected. If the bottom six strips contain no interval, or the selected chain has fewer than three intervals, the result is \texttt{lost}.

Figure~\ref{fig:c6_chain} shows the association on the mask of Figure~\ref{fig:geometric_strips_projection}. The blob at the bottom left has more than 500 pixels, so the cleaning does not remove it, and it gives one interval in each of strips 0 to 4. Strip 0 contains two intervals, and the one on the left, in the blob, is tried first as a seed. Its chain accepts the blob intervals up to strip 4. The only interval of strip 5 belongs to the corridor, and it is shifted by 113~px from the interval accepted in strip 4, well beyond the jump limit of 28~px. The same happens in the next strips, so the chain finds no other candidate and ends at strip 4. The corridor interval of strip 0 is not in this chain, so a second chain is grown from it. This chain accepts one interval in each strip up to strip 13. Strips 14 and 15 contain no interval, and the chain ends at the top of the ROI. In the bottom six strips, no interval is left outside the two chains, so no other seed is available and no other chain is grown (Figure~\ref{fig:c6_chain}a). The corridor chain has 28 samples and the blob chain has 10, so the corridor chain is selected, although its total cost is higher, 122 against 26.8 (Figure~\ref{fig:c6_chain}b). In each of these strips, the corridor chain compares them with its last accepted interval: they are shifted by 96 to 136~px, well beyond the jump limit of 28--29~px, and they are rejected. They are not in any chain, and their endpoints are not used in the edge fits.

\subsection{Separate fitting of the corridor edges}
\label{sec:c6_fitting}

The two edges are fitted separately. The left edge uses the left endpoints $\ell_i$ of the accepted intervals, and the right edge uses the right endpoints $r_i$. Clipped endpoints are not used, so an interval clipped on one side gives a sample only to the other edge, and the two edges can have samples in different strips. The row of a sample is the center of its strip. In the equations below, a sample of one edge is the point $(y_i,x_i)$, where $y_i$ is its row and $x_i$ is the endpoint, $\ell$ for the left edge and $r$ for the right edge. In these equations $i$ indexes the samples of one edge, not the intervals. Each edge needs at least two samples. If one edge has fewer, the result is \texttt{lost}, because the centerline is never estimated from a single edge. The edges are first fitted inside a crossing check, described at the end of this section, and then once more on the intervals that remain.

Each edge is fitted with iteratively reweighted least squares. The first fit uses uniform weights, and ten reweighting iterations follow. At each iteration, the distance of each sample from the current line gives its new weight, and the line is fitted again. A sample far from the line gets a low weight. How fast the weight decreases is set by the residual scale $\sigma$, a parameter of the extractor. A sample at distance $\sigma$ from the line gets weight 0.5. On the robot, $\sigma=18$~px (Section~\ref{sec:c6_parameter_setting}). At iteration $t$, the residuals and the new weights are
\begin{equation}
    e_i^{(t)}=x_i-\bigl(m^{(t)}y_i+q^{(t)}\bigr),\qquad
    w_i^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{1+(e_i^{(t)}/\sigma)^2},
    \label{eq:c6_reweight}
\end{equation}
and the line is fitted again by weighted least squares, with each weight applied to the residual before it is squared:
\begin{equation}
    (m,q)=\mathop{\arg\min}_{m,q}
        \sum_i \left[w_i\bigl(x_i-(my_i+q)\bigr)\right]^2.
    \label{eq:c6_objective}
\end{equation}
Each squared residual is therefore weighted by $w_i^2$. The weights are not accumulated across iterations, since they are computed again from the current line only. The reweighting reduces the effect of isolated outliers, but not of many samples from outside the corridor, because they lie on a consistent line and the fit follows them. Figure~\ref{fig:robust_edge_fitting} shows the reweighting on the samples of the selected chain of Figure~\ref{fig:c6_chain}b.

The two edges of a corridor converge upward in the image, but in the rows where the chain has samples they are as far apart as the corridor is wide. Every accepted interval is at least $d_{\min}=8$~px wide, the minimum interval width of Section~\ref{sec:c6_candidates}. If the fitted edges come closer than $d_{\min}$ in these rows, the intervals above the row where this happens do not follow the corridor. They can come, for example, from a region attached to the corridor, and they bend the fits. The edges are therefore checked before the final fit. With $\Delta m=m_R-m_L$ and $\Delta q=q_R-q_L$, the modeled width is
\begin{equation}
    D(y)=x_R(y)-x_L(y)=\Delta m\,y+\Delta q,
    \label{eq:c6_width}
\end{equation}
the signed horizontal distance between the two fitted lines at row $y$. It is negative where the lines have crossed. $D(y)$ is not the width of an interval, and it is defined at every row, also in the rows where the chain has no interval. The lines are fitted on the left and right endpoints. At the row of an interval $[\ell,r]$ not clipped on either side, a good fit therefore gives $D$ close to $r-\ell=d-1$, not to the interval width $d$. When $\Delta m\neq0$, $D(y)$ equals $d_{\min}$ at one value of $y$ only,
\begin{equation}
    y_\times=\frac{d_{\min}-\Delta q}{\Delta m}.
    \label{eq:c6_crossing}
\end{equation}
In general $y_\times$ is not an integer row. The edges converge upward when $\Delta m>0$, in the code when $\Delta m>10^{-6}$, and then $D(y)<d_{\min}$ above $y_\times$. If the edges converge upward and $y_\times$ is below the highest sample, the intervals whose strip center is above $y_\times$ are discarded, together with their samples on both edges, and both edges are fitted again. This crossing check is made at most three times. Each pass fits the edges, checks them, and makes at most one discard. After a discard in the third pass, the edges fitted on the remaining intervals are not checked again here. The check stops earlier in three cases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The check finds no crossing to treat: the edges do not converge upward, or $y_\times$ is not below the highest sample.
    \item The discard would leave fewer than three intervals. The intervals are then kept, and the check stops with the crossing still present.
    \item An edge has fewer than two samples, and the result is \texttt{lost}. The previous rule counts only the intervals, so this can also happen after a discard, when the remaining intervals are clipped on that side.
\end{itemize}
If the result is not \texttt{lost}, the final fit uses the remaining intervals. A crossing in the rows where the chain has samples can therefore still be present in three cases: when too few intervals would remain, after a discard in the third pass, or when the edges do not converge upward, which the check does not treat. These crossings are found by the width check of Section~\ref{sec:c6_output}.

\subsection{Validation, confidence, and output}
\label{sec:c6_output}

After the final fit, the width check tests the final edges. It finds the crossings that are still present in the three cases listed at the end of Section~\ref{sec:c6_fitting}. It also defines the valid range, and it rejects edges that do not converge upward and a valid range that is too short. The check uses the modeled width $D(y)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:c6_width} and $y_\times$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:c6_crossing}, both computed on the final edges. It looks at the rows from the highest sample used in the final fit down to the bottom row of the image, row $H-1$. The valid range is the part of these rows where $D(y)\geq d_{\min}$. It starts at the highest sample, or at $y_\times$ when $y_\times$ lies just below it, within the tolerance described below, and it always ends at the bottom row of the image.

The robot moves between parallel rows, so because of perspective the edges of the corridor converge upward, with $\Delta m>0$. Edges that are parallel or converge downward indicate a wrong fit, and the check fails.

When the edges converge upward, $D(y)$ is below $d_{\min}$ in the rows above $y_\times$, so these rows are not in the valid range. The check then works as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $y_\times$ is at the highest sample or above it, no row is removed, and the valid range starts at the highest sample.
    \item If $y_\times$ is below the highest sample by at most $0.05H$, that is 11.2~px on the robot, a little more than the strip height of 10~px, the valid range starts at $y_\times$. This tolerance accepts a crossing just below the highest sample.
    \item If $y_\times$ is more than $0.05H$ below the highest sample, the check fails. The edges are then closer than $d_{\min}$ in rows where the chain has samples, which indicates a chain that joins different regions or a wrong fit.
    \item The check also fails if the distance from the top of the valid range to the bottom row of the image is less than 10\% of the ROI height $H-y_0$. On the robot the ROI has 160 rows, so the limit is 16~px. The corridor is then seen in too few rows.
\end{itemize}

The confidence $C$ combines four scores between 0 and 1. They use the seed and the accepted intervals of the selected chain that were not discarded by the crossing check of Section~\ref{sec:c6_fitting}. Below they are called the intervals of the chain.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tracking $T$.} The fraction of the strips of the valid range that contain an interval of the chain. It is the number of intervals of the chain whose strip center lies in the valid range, divided by the number of strips whose center lies in the valid range. The intervals of the chain above the valid range are not counted, for example the interval of the highest sample when the valid range starts at $y_\times$ within the tolerance. $T$ is 1 when every strip of the valid range has an interval of the chain, and it is low when the chain skips many of them. For example, a chain from strip 0 to strip 13 that skips two strips below strip 11 has 12 intervals. If the crossing check discards the intervals of strips 12 and 13, 10 intervals remain. If the interval of strip 11 gives at least one sample, and the final edges converge upward and reach $d_{\min}$ at or above its center, the valid range starts at the center of strip 11, the highest remaining sample. Then $T=10/12\approx0.83$: the two skipped strips lower $T$, while the two discarded intervals lie above the valid range and do not.
    \item \textbf{Fit $F$.} How close the samples are to the fitted edge lines. With $\bar e$ the mean horizontal distance of the samples of both edges from their lines, $F=\operatorname{clip}(1-\bar e/\sigma,0,1)$. $F$ is 1 when the samples lie on the lines and falls to 0 when $\bar e$ reaches $\sigma=18$~px. It is low when the edges of the mask are irregular or when some samples come from outside the corridor. The distances are not weighted, so a sample with a low weight in the fit still counts fully in $\bar e$.
    \item \textbf{Width regularity $A$.} With straight edges, the width of the corridor changes by the same amount from one strip to the next. $A$ measures how far the widths are from this. It uses the widths $r_i-\ell_i$ of the intervals of the chain that are not clipped on either side, from the bottom up, since the width of a clipped interval is not the corridor width. The error $E_D$ is computed in three steps:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The changes of width between consecutive intervals are computed, and then the changes between consecutive changes, the second differences. A second difference is zero when the width changes by the same amount twice.
        \item The 90th percentile of the absolute second differences is taken. With few intervals it is close to the largest one.
        \item It is divided by the median width, so that $E_D$ is a jump relative to the width of the corridor.
    \end{enumerate}
    Then $A=\operatorname{clip}(1-E_D/0.30,0,1)$, which is 1 when the width changes by the same amount at every step and 0 when $E_D$ reaches 0.30. For example, the widths 120, 114, 108, 102 and 96~px change by $-6$~px at every step, so the second differences are zero, $E_D=0$ and $A=1$. If a region attached to the corridor widens the third interval to 140~px, the changes are $-6$, $+26$, $-38$ and $-6$~px, and the absolute second differences are 32, 64 and 32~px. Their 90th percentile is 57.6~px and the median width is 114~px, so $E_D=0.51$ and $A=0$. A skipped strip or an excluded clipped interval between two widths also gives a nonzero second difference when the width changes along the corridor. With fewer than three unclipped intervals there is no second difference and the width cannot be checked, so $A$ is 0.
    \item \textbf{Gaps $G$.} The longest hole in the chain. With $g_{\max}$ the longest run of strips skipped between two intervals of the chain, $G=\operatorname{clip}(1-g_{\max}/3,0,1)$. $G$ is 1 when the chain skips no strip and 0 when it skips three strips in a row, the longest gap the chain can cross. Every strip of the valid range without an interval of the chain lowers $T$, also below the seed. $G$ depends only on the longest run of skipped strips between two intervals of the chain, so one long gap lowers $G$ more than several short ones.
\end{itemize}
The confidence is their weighted sum:
\begin{equation}
    C=0.3125\,T+0.3125\,F+0.25\,A+0.125\,G.
    \label{eq:c6_confidence4}
\end{equation}
The result is \texttt{lost} if the width check fails, if $\bar e>2\sigma$, or if $C<0.38$. Otherwise it is \texttt{degraded} for $C<0.60$ and \texttt{ok} for $C\geq0.60$. $C$ is a heuristic score, not a calibrated probability.

For a \texttt{lost} result, no centerline model is returned. Otherwise, the centerline model $x_C(y)$ is returned. The extractor also returns a mask of the size of the input. The centerline is drawn in it with one pixel per row, from the top of the valid range, rounded up to an integer row, to the bottom row of the image, and then dilated with a $3\times3$ elliptical kernel. For a \texttt{lost} result this mask is empty. The controller reads the lateral error from the model, not from the mask. It evaluates the model at the reference row $y_{\mathrm{ref}}=156$ (Section~\ref{subsubsec:control_node}), also when this row is above the valid range.

        Percentile of the second differences & 90 \\
        Width-regularity scale & 0.30 \\
        Weights of $T$, $F$, $A$, $G$ in $C$ & 0.3125, 0.3125, 0.25, 0.125 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabularx}
\end{table}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ROI start $\rho$.} It sets the requested first row of the ROI, $\lfloor\rho H\rfloor$, which is then extended up to $y_0=H-Nh$ (Section~\ref{sec:c6_candidates}). It should be the highest row where the path is visible. A smaller $\rho$ adds rows above the path, whose regions can enter the chain and pull the fits. With a larger $\rho$, the edges are fitted on fewer rows of the corridor. With the same $N$, $\rho$ can also change the strip height $h$. On the robot, $\rho=0.30$, chosen on the development data (Section~\ref{subsubsec:c8_parameters}).
    \item \textbf{Number of strips $N$.} The ROI is divided into $N$ strips of height $h$. More strips give more samples to the edge fits, but a thin strip is emptied or split more easily by a hole in the mask. The endpoints of an interval are the outermost path columns over all the $h$ rows of its strip. In a thick strip, noise on the edge in any one of these rows moves an endpoint outward, and so does the inclination of the edge, by up to half the shift of the edge over the strip. The seed window, the bottom six strips (Section~\ref{sec:c6_selection}), and the number of consecutive skipped strips allowed, three, are set in strips and not in rows. On the robot, with $N=16$ and $h=10$, they cover 60 and 30 rows. With $N=32$ and $h=5$ they would cover 30 and 15 rows, so they have to be changed together with $N$ to keep the same rows.
    \item \textbf{Merging gap.} The largest number of inactive columns between two runs merged into one interval. It should be larger than the holes and shadows inside the corridor and smaller than the crop row between two adjacent corridors, which is narrowest at the top of the ROI. If it is too large, a blob or an adjacent corridor is merged into the interval and moves its endpoint. On the robot it is 30~px, chosen on the development data (Section~\ref{subsubsec:c8_parameters}).
    \item \textbf{Minimum component area and minimum interval width $d_{\min}$.} The cleaning removes the components with fewer pixels than the minimum area, and the intervals narrower than $d_{\min}$ are discarded. The area should be below that of the parts of the corridor separated from the rest, and $d_{\min}$ below the narrowest width of the corridor, at the top of the ROI. Smaller values keep blobs and narrow intervals of noise, which can start or enter a chain. $d_{\min}$ is also the threshold on $D(y)$ in the crossing check and in the width check, so a larger $d_{\min}$ discards more intervals and shortens the valid range. On the robot they are 500 pixels and 8~px.
    \item \textbf{Jump limit $\Delta_{\max}$.} The largest allowed shift of each endpoint between the last accepted interval and a candidate, $\max(28,\,0.5\,d_p)$~px (Section~\ref{sec:c6_selection}). It should be above the shift of the corridor edges, also over four strips when three strips are skipped, and below the shift to the regions next to the corridor. If it is too small, the chain stops on inclined edges or where the interval widens. If it is too large, the regions next to the corridor become candidates. The shift of an edge is its slope times the number of rows. The slope of an edge depends on the heading and the lateral offset of the robot in the row and on the pitch and roll of the camera.
    \item \textbf{Residual scale $\sigma$.} A sample at distance $\sigma$ from the edge line gets weight 0.5 (Eq.~\eqref{eq:c6_reweight}). It should be above the distance of the correct samples from the line, which comes from the irregular edges of the mask, and below the distance of the outliers. A smaller $\sigma$ lowers the weight of correct samples and the fit score $F$, so more results are \texttt{degraded} or \texttt{lost}. A larger $\sigma$ keeps the outliers in the fit and accepts more wrong results, because $F$ and the threshold $2\sigma$ grow. On the robot, $\sigma=18$~px.
    \item \textbf{Status thresholds.} The result is \texttt{lost} if the width check fails, if $\bar e>2\sigma$ or if $C<0.38$. Otherwise it is \texttt{degraded} for $C<0.60$ and \texttt{ok} for $C\geq0.60$ (Section~\ref{sec:c6_output}). A higher lost threshold rejects more frames, and a higher ok threshold makes more results \texttt{degraded}, which the controller filters differently (Section~\ref{subsubsec:control_node}).
\end{itemize}
These values are set for the camera, its mounting and the fields of this work. With another camera, mounting or crop, the parameters have to be set again for the new scenario. This is a limitation of \textit{Strip-IRLS}, and one of the reasons for \textit{EdgeNet} (Section~\ref{sec:c6_network}).

\section{EdgeNet, an edge regression network}
\label{sec:c6_network}

\textit{EdgeNet} is a first test of replacing the geometric steps of \textit{Strip-IRLS} with a learned model. Like the second network of M{\"u}ller et al.~\cite{muller2018driving} (Section~\ref{subsec:bilateral_vs_centroids}), it takes a segmentation mask and not the image, but it regresses the two corridor edges instead of waypoints. Its aim is to avoid the geometric parameters of \textit{Strip-IRLS}, which have to be set by hand for each scenario (Section~\ref{sec:c6_parameter_setting}). The network has no geometric parameters in the extraction. To adapt it to a new scenario, the generator of its training masks is changed and the network is trained again, instead of tuning thresholds. It still has the validity and status thresholds described below.

The network is a small CNN of about 0.8 million parameters, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:c6_edgenet}. Its input is the $224\times224$ mask, and it has five outputs: the $x$ positions of the left and right edges at rows 156 and 223, and the logit of the probability $p_{\mathrm{valid}}$ that the mask contains a corridor. Row 156 is the row read by the controller and row 223 is the last row of the image. Each edge line is the line through its predicted points at rows 156 and 223, and the centerline is the mean of the two lines, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:c6_line}.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \input{images/architectures/edgenet_tikz}%
    \caption{Architecture of \textit{EdgeNet}. Each box is a feature map, colored by the layer that produces it and labeled with channels $\times$ height $\times$ width. The two maps of a block have the same size and share one label. The thickness of a box grows with the channels and its height with the size of the map, not to scale. The mask is averaged over $2\times2$ blocks, and two channels with the $x$ and $y$ coordinates of each pixel, scaled to $[-1,1]$, are added, so the convolutions also see where each pixel is. Each of the four blocks halves the size with a stride-2 convolution and then applies a second convolution. The $96\times7\times7$ map is flattened and passed to two fully connected layers, the first followed by ReLU and dropout 0.1.}
    \label{fig:c6_edgenet}
\end{figure}

The confidence $C$ combines two values. The first is $p_{\mathrm{valid}}$, given by the network: its estimate of the probability that the mask contains a corridor. The network learns it from the validity label of the training masks, defined below. The second is computed by the extractor after the network: the intersection over union (IoU) between the region enclosed by the two edge lines and the path pixels of the mask, in the rows from 64 to the bottom of the image. The mask is not cleaned as in \textit{Strip-IRLS}. With $R$ the set of pixels between the two lines and $P$ the set of path pixels, both in the rows from 64 to 223,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{IoU}=\frac{|R\cap P|}{|R\cup P|}=\frac{|R\cap P|}{|R|+|P|-|R\cap P|},
    \label{eq:c6_edgenet_iou}
\end{equation}
where $|\cdot|$ is the number of pixels of a set. It is the IoU of Eq.~\eqref{eq:iou}, with the region between the lines in place of the prediction and the path pixels of the mask in place of the reference. The numerator counts the path pixels between the lines. The denominator also counts the pixels between the lines that are not path and the path pixels outside the lines. The IoU is 1 when the region between the lines covers exactly the path of the mask. It is lower when the lines are wrong, or when the mask also contains path outside them, for example an adjacent corridor. The confidence is their product, $C=p_{\mathrm{valid}}\cdot\mathrm{IoU}$. The status is \texttt{lost} if $p_{\mathrm{valid}}<0.5$, if the distance between the two predicted edges at row 223 is below 8~px, or if $C<0.38$. Otherwise it is \texttt{degraded} for $C<0.60$ and \texttt{ok} for $C\geq0.60$. The width is checked at row 223 and not at row 156 because of the labels of the training masks, described below.

The network is trained only on synthetic masks, with labels computed from the corridor model and not from an extractor, so that it does not learn the errors of an algorithm. M{\"u}ller et al.\ obtain realistic errors by applying a real segmentation network to simulated images. Here the errors of the segmentation are modeled by hand in the generator. The generator draws perspective corridors whose apex, the row where the two edges meet, goes from above the image down to row 190. A quarter of the corridors are curved. The generator adds disturbances found in predicted masks, such as irregular edges, adjacent corridors, holes and blobs. Above the apex the labels extend the edge lines, so when the apex is below row 156 the labeled edges are swapped at that row. For this reason the width is checked at row 223. Each training mask also has a validity label. It is 1 when the corridor drawn before the disturbances has at least 800 pixels and at least one of its two edge lines lies inside the image, more than 2~px from the border, in at least 15 rows from row 64. It is 0 for the other masks and for the masks without a corridor. The label does not depend on the disturbances, so a corridor partly erased by them is still valid. About 14\% of the 300\,000 training masks have no valid corridor. The generator does not use the synthetic test sets of Chapter~\ref{cap:experiments}, but it was written by the same author, who knew their scenarios. The loss is a smooth L1 loss on the four edge positions, plus twice the same loss on the centerline at row 156, both only on masks with a valid corridor, plus 0.2 times the binary cross-entropy on the validity. On the 17\,266 masks with a valid corridor among 20\,000 synthetic validation masks, the median centerline error at row 156 is 1.7~px and the 90th percentile 13.0~px. These masks were also used to choose the checkpoint with the lowest 90th percentile. These values say nothing about real masks. The network is exported to ONNX and run on the robot with ONNX Runtime.

\section{Baseline extractors}
\label{sec:c6_baseline_algorithms}

\textit{Strip-IRLS} and \textit{EdgeNet} are compared with the three geometric baselines of this section.

\subsection{Center points}
\label{subsec:c6_center_baseline}

The \textit{Center points} extractor uses the same mask cleaning and interval extraction as \textit{Strip-IRLS}, with the same merging gap of 30 columns. Its ROI is built as in \textit{Strip-IRLS} (Section~\ref{sec:c6_candidates}), with $\rho=0.30$ and $N=24$ strips. The strips are $h=\lceil 157/24\rceil=7$~px high, so the ROI is extended upward from row 67 to row $y_0=H-Nh=56$. Each interval is reduced to its midpoint $c=(\ell+r)/2$ and its width $d=r-\ell+1$. The seed is the interval of the bottom six strips with the most path pixels, with ties broken by width. Intervals are associated upward with the cost
\begin{equation}
    J_i = |c_i - c_p| + 0.4\, |d_i - d_p|,
    \label{eq:c6_center_cost}
\end{equation}
with $c_p$ and $d_p$ the midpoint and width of the last accepted interval, under the jump limit $|c_i - c_p| \le \max(28, 0.5\, d_p)$, with the same stopping rule and the same minimum of three intervals as \textit{Strip-IRLS}.

One line is fitted to the midpoints with four iterations of the weights of Eq.~\eqref{eq:c6_reweight}. Unlike \textit{Strip-IRLS}, the initial weights are proportional to the pixel count of each interval (normalized to mean one), the weights multiply across iterations, and clipped intervals are not excluded. The confidence combines the same four scores as \textit{Strip-IRLS} (Section~\ref{sec:c6_output}), tracking $T$, fit $F$, regularity of the width $A$ and gaps $G$, with weights 0.30, 0.35, 0.25 and 0.10. $T$ is the number of accepted intervals divided by the number of all the strips, so the strips above the end of the corridor also lower it. $A$ uses the widths of all the accepted intervals, and $G$ also counts the skipped strips that end the association. The result is \texttt{lost} if the mean residual exceeds $2\sigma$ or if the confidence is below 0.38, and \texttt{degraded} if it is below 0.60. Because the midpoints are formed before the fit, a disturbance on one edge moves the midpoint and enters the centerline.

\subsection{Column histogram}
\label{subsec:c6_histogram_baseline}

The \textit{Column histogram} extractor is a simplified version of the column projection of SegMin. SegMin sums a plant mask, accumulated over recent frames and refined with depth, along the image columns, smooths the profile, and takes its minimum~\cite{navone2025gpsfree}. \textit{Column histogram} uses the path mask of a single frame, without depth or smoothing, and takes the maximum. Its ROI starts at $y_0=\lfloor 0.30H\rfloor=67$, and the mask is cleaned with the same opening and closing as \textit{Strip-IRLS}. The connected components with fewer than 300 pixels are then removed. In each column, the extractor counts the path pixels of the cleaned ROI, and the target column $x_{\text{target}}$ is the column with the most path pixels. If several columns have the maximum count, as when the corridor fills the whole height of the ROI, $x_{\text{target}}$ is the center of the widest group of adjacent ones. If two or more groups have the same largest width, no column is chosen and the result is \texttt{lost}. The confidence is the maximum count divided by the ROI height $H-y_0$, with the same thresholds as \textit{Strip-IRLS} (0.38 and 0.60). The output is the vertical line $x=x_{\text{target}}$, so the method gives a lateral position but no heading.

\subsection{RANSAC edges}
\label{subsec:c6_ransac_baseline}

RANSAC line fitting is used in agricultural row detection on points of the crop rows~\cite{bai2023vision,sensors2025deeplabv3}. The RANSAC extractor applies it to the edges of the segmented corridor, without strips or bottom-up association.

The ROI starts at $y_0=\lfloor 0.30H\rfloor=67$ and the mask is cleaned with the same opening and closing as \textit{Strip-IRLS}. The extractor then keeps one connected component, a large one that starts low in the image. In each ROI row of this component, its leftmost and rightmost pixels are edge points. Rows where they are less than 10~px apart are skipped. As in \textit{Strip-IRLS}, the endpoints at the image border are not used.

Each edge is fitted with RANSAC~\cite{fischler1981ransac}: in 100 trials, two random points define a line, and the points within 18~px of it are inliers. The line with the most inliers is refitted by least squares on its inliers, and the centerline is the mean of the two edge lines, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:c6_line}. The random draws use a fixed seed, so the same mask always gives the same line.

The result is \texttt{lost} if one edge has fewer than 10 points or its points span fewer than 20 rows, for example when the mask is a band of path across the bottom of the image at the end of a row. It is also \texttt{lost} if the component has fewer than 1000 pixels in the ROI, or if the mean fraction of inliers of the two edges is below 0.30. Otherwise a score combines the fraction of inliers, their residual, the vertical extent of the component and the regularity of its width. Below 0.38 the result is \texttt{lost}, below 0.60 \texttt{degraded}, and from 0.60 \texttt{ok}.

\textit{Strip-IRLS} has three structural limitations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Seed dependence.} The seeds are searched only in the bottom six strips. If these contain no interval, the result is \texttt{lost} even if the upper part of the corridor is segmented. If a hole wider than the merging gap and taller than these strips splits the bottom of the corridor, the chain follows only one part (Section~\ref{subsubsec:geometric_comparison}). At a fork above these strips, the chain takes the branch with the lowest cost and never revises it.
    \item \textbf{Number of parameters.} \textit{Strip-IRLS} has 16 arguments and 13 constants (Table~\ref{tab:c6_defaults}), most of them in pixels of the mask, and several act together. They were set for the camera, its mounting and the fields of this work, and have to be set again for a new scenario (Section~\ref{sec:c6_parameter_setting}).
    \item \textbf{Missing plants on one side.} The interval widens on that side. Within the jump limit it is accepted, and its endpoint on that side is a wrong sample that only the reweighting can reduce. Beyond the limit the strip is skipped. If the interval reaches the image border, its endpoint on that side gives no sample.
\end{itemize}

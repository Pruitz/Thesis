\chapter{State of the Art}
\label{cap:stato_arte}

This chapter reviews the works related to the parts of this thesis: the
segmentation network, the annotation of the images used to train it, the
extraction of the navigation reference from the mask, and the controller
that steers the robot with this reference. Each extraction method is linked to
the extractor of Chapter~\ref{cap:geometric_reference} that is based on
it or compared with it.

\section{Segmentation for row navigation}
\label{sec:learning_based_segmentation}

Classical pipelines combine image filtering, thresholding, and
application-specific geometric rules. Mendez et al. convert vineyard
images to grayscale and apply Gaussian filtering and Otsu thresholding.
They keep the largest white blob, which is the sky above the row, and
find the row center from it (Section~\ref{subsec:histogram_methods})
\cite[Section~3.4]{mendez2023vineyards}. Such methods can be
effective in the conditions for which their parameters were selected.
However, fixed thresholds are sensitive to changes in illumination, and
the complexity of the field and the variety of crops make it difficult
to segment the image from low-level features alone \cite{bai2023vision}.

Semantic segmentation assigns a class to each pixel, and the choice of
the classes decides what the geometric stage can recover. De Silva et
al.\ train U-Net to predict crop-row masks in sugar-beet images. In
their labels each crop row is a single white line 6~pixels wide, not a
set of individual plants~\cite[Sections~3.3 and~4, Figure~5]{silva2023rowdetection}.
Navone et al.\ predict a binary vegetation mask from RGB images with a
MobileNetV3 backbone and an LR-ASPP head, and then combine this mask with
depth to extract the reference \cite{navone2023ecmr,navone2025gpsfree}.
Li et al.\ segment the inter-row path instead of the
plants~\cite{li2022multiroi}.

\section{Segmentation architectures}
\label{sec:segmentation_architectures}

Fully Convolutional Networks (FCN) convert the fully connected layers of
a classification network into convolutions, so the network outputs a
map of class scores at a much lower resolution than the input image. A
deconvolution layer then upsamples this map
to one prediction per pixel~\cite[Sections~3.1 and~4.1]{long2015fcn}. DeepLabV3 uses atrous convolutions and
atrous spatial pyramid pooling to capture multi-scale context without
decreasing the feature resolution~\cite{chen2017deeplab}. For embedded
applications with limited computing resources, Howard et
al.~\cite{howard2019mobilenetv3} introduced MobileNetV3, whose blocks are
built on depthwise convolutions, together with the Lite Reduced Atrous
Spatial Pyramid Pooling (LR-ASPP) segmentation head, which uses a large
pooling kernel and only one $1\times1$ convolution in its pooling branch.
Chapter~\ref{cap:architectures} describes the three networks.

Transfer learning reuses most of the layers of a network trained on a
similar task and replaces the top ones. It speeds up training
considerably and requires significantly less training data. The reused
layers can first be frozen and then fine-tuned, that is, trained further
on the new task with a reduced learning
rate~\cite[Chapter~11]{geron2026handson}. Long et al.\ obtain their
segmentation networks by fine-tuning classifiers pretrained on
ILSVRC~\cite[Section~4]{long2015fcn}. In agriculture, Li et al.\ train
their network on 2000 maize images and use the resulting weights as the
pretrained weights for separate datasets of 1000 tomato, cucumber, and
wheat images~\cite[Abstract, Sections~2.1.1 and~2.2.2]{li2022multiroi}.
Navone et al.\ initialize their segmentation model with ImageNet weights
and train it on synthetic AgriSeg images and 100 real crop
images~\cite[Section~4.1, Table~1]{navone2025gpsfree}. In this thesis,
the three networks start from the pretrained segmentation weights of
TorchVision and are fine-tuned in two stages
(Section~\ref{subsec:c4_training_config}).

\section{Annotation of the training images}
\label{sec:dataset_annotation}

Manual pixel-level annotation is slow and tedious, and a dataset that
covers several crops, growth stages, and lighting conditions needs many
annotated images. The first two Segment Anything models, SAM and SAM~2,
segment a single object for each prompt, given as points, boxes, or
masks, so the object to segment has to be indicated in each image, for
example with a click. SAM~3
segments all the instances of a concept, given as a short noun phrase,
as image exemplars, or as both~\cite[Section~1]{carion2026sam3}. In this thesis, SAM~3 labels the
training images offline from text prompts, and its masks are used
without manual correction (Sections~\ref{sec:sam3_theory}
and~\ref{subsec:c4_sam_annotation}).

\section{Extraction of the navigation reference}
\label{sec:geometric_extraction}

The geometric stage reduces a mask to a quantity the controller can use.
In the methods reviewed below, this is an image column, one or more
straight lines, or a curve. Chapter~\ref{cap:geometric_reference} describes five extractors:
\textit{Strip-IRLS} and \textit{EdgeNet}, developed in this work, and
three baselines, \textit{Center points}, \textit{Column histogram}, and
\textit{RANSAC edges}. Each subsection below describes one family of
methods and the extractors related to it.

\subsection{Column histograms}
\label{subsec:histogram_methods}

Histogram methods aggregate mask pixels by image column. Mendez et al.
estimate the row center from the five columns with the largest pixel
counts in the thresholded sky region
\cite[Section~3.4.3]{mendez2023vineyards}. In SegMin, Navone et al.\ sum
along the columns a vegetation mask combined over recent frames and
refined with depth, and take the column with the least
vegetation~\cite[Section~3]{navone2025gpsfree}. \textit{Column
histogram} (Section~\ref{subsec:c5_histogram_baseline}) is a simplified
version of SegMin.

\subsection{Horizontal strips and interval midpoints}
\label{subsec:strip_methods}

Li et al. combine a Faster-U-net inter-row-path mask with an adaptive
multi-ROI procedure. Horizontal regions supply the midpoints of the
path, which are used as control points for a multisegment cubic
B-spline~\cite[Section~2.2.3]{li2022multiroi}.

\textit{Center points} (Section~\ref{subsec:c5_center_baseline}) follows
this scheme and fits one straight line to the interval midpoints.
\textit{Strip-IRLS} uses the same horizontal strips but fits the two
edges of the corridor separately.

\subsection{RANSAC line fitting}
\label{subsec:ransac_methods}

RANSAC is among the line fitting algorithms used to extract navigation
lines in agricultural vision~\cite{bai2023vision}. Wu et al.\ extract
crop-row anchor points from the segmentation masks, group them with
DBSCAN clustering, and fit navigation lines with
RANSAC~\cite{sensors2025deeplabv3}. \textit{RANSAC edges} (Section~\ref{subsec:c5_ransac_baseline}) applies
RANSAC to the row extremes of the path corridor instead of to points of
the crop rows.

\subsection{Robust line fitting with IRLS}
\label{subsec:irls_methods}

Kneip et al. detect crop--ground transitions in the scanlines of a
stereo elevation map, then fit a linear crop-edge model using a Huber
M-estimator and iteratively reweighted least
squares~\cite[Sections~5--7]{kneip2020cropedge}.

\textit{Strip-IRLS} (Section~\ref{sec:c5_fitting}) also fits lines by
iteratively reweighted least squares, but in image space, on the two
edges of the path corridor, and with a weight function different from
the Huber function of Kneip et al.

\section{Control and evaluation}
\label{sec:system_integration}

Navigation systems convert the extracted reference into motion commands.
Navone et al.\ and Mendez et al.\ both compute the angular velocity from
the horizontal offset of the row center from the image center. Navone et
al.\ also reduce the linear velocity as the offset grows and smooth both
commands with an exponential moving
average~\cite[Section~3.4]{navone2025gpsfree}. Mendez et al.\ keep the
linear velocity constant, use a proportional gain, and switch to a
second mode based on the ratio of sky pixels on the two sides of the row
center~\cite[Section~3.5]{mendez2023vineyards}. The
controller of this thesis also filters the error with an exponential
moving average and uses a proportional steering law
(Section~\ref{subsubsec:control_node}).

Mendez et al. report closed-loop vineyard trials and measure the
centering error from the distances to the two sides of the row, given by
a LiDAR that is not used for navigation
\cite[Sections~3.6 and~4]{mendez2023vineyards}. Navone et al.\ compare
the trajectory with the central path of the row in simulation. In crop
fields, they measure the lateral displacement with a LiDAR and the
heading with an attitude and heading reference
system~\cite[Sections~4.3--4.4]{navone2025gpsfree}.
In the field tests of this thesis, the position of the robot is measured
with a total station (Chapter~\ref{cap:experiments}).

\section{Positioning of this thesis}
\label{sec:thesis_positioning}

This thesis builds a row-following system without GNSS, with one RGB
camera and the onboard computer of the robot. Its main choices are the
following:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Automatic labels.} SAM~3 generated, from text prompts, the
    masks used to train the segmentation network that runs on the robot.
    The masks were used without manual correction
    (Section~\ref{subsec:c4_sam_annotation}).

    \item \textbf{Path segmentation.} The network segments the path between
    the rows and not the plants.

    \item \textbf{Two path extractors.} \textit{Strip-IRLS} fits the two edges of the corridor separately instead of
    using the midpoints of the path intervals
    (Section~\ref{sec:c5_stripirls}). \textit{EdgeNet}, still at an
    early stage, is a small network that regresses the two edges directly
    from the mask (Section~\ref{sec:c5_network}). Both are compared with
    three baselines adapted from the methods of
    Section~\ref{sec:geometric_extraction}: \textit{Center points},
    \textit{Column histogram}, and \textit{RANSAC edges}
    (Section~\ref{sec:c5_baseline_algorithms}).
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Robot Platform and Integration}
\label{cap:platform}

This chapter describes the hardware and software components of the robot platform used for field validation. It covers the camera configuration, computing hardware, software architecture using ROS, and the deployment of the trained segmentation model for real-time inference.

\section{Robot hardware and sensors}
\label{sec:hardware}

\subsection{Robot platform}
\label{subsec:robot_platform}

The experimental platform is a custom-built mobile robot prototype, named Demetra, developed for agricultural inspection and autonomous navigation in row-crop environments. The chassis features a four-wheel steering (4WS) and four-wheel drive (4WD) kinematic configuration. All four wheels are independently driven, and both the front and rear axles provide active steering.

Steering operates in a symmetric contra-steering mode (opposite-phase steering), where the front wheels steer by an angle $\delta_f = \delta$ and the rear wheels steer by an equal and opposite angle $\delta_r = -\delta$. This configuration constrains the instantaneous center of rotation (ICR) to lie on the transverse axis passing through the geometric center of the chassis ($x = 0$). Relative to a conventional front-wheel Ackermann steering model with wheelbase $L$, symmetric four-wheel contra-steering halves the turning radius from $R = L / \tan\delta$ to:
\begin{equation}
    R = \frac{L}{2 \tan\delta}.
    \label{eq:turning_radius_4ws}
\end{equation}
This reduction in turning radius enables tight turns at row headlands without requiring the robot to swing wide into adjacent crop beds. In addition, the vehicle sideslip angle at the center of geometry remains zero under ideal kinematic rolling conditions ($\beta = 0$), so the heading of the chassis aligns with the tangent to the path.

The traction subsystem comprises four DJI RoboMaster M3508 brushless DC motors, each coupled to an integrated 19:1 planetary gearbox. Operating at a nominal voltage of 24~V, each motor unit delivers up to 3.0~N$\cdot$m of continuous torque with a no-load output shaft speed of 482~rpm (peak torque up to 5.0~N$\cdot$m). Speed regulation is handled by four individual DJI RoboMaster C620 electronic speed controllers (ESCs) implementing field-oriented control (FOC) over a CAN bus interface at 1~Mbps, with continuous current limits of 20~A per channel.

Active steering is actuated by four CubeMars AK70-10 KV100 robotic actuators. Each steering unit integrates a brushless DC motor, a 10:1 planetary gearhead, a 14-bit magnetic absolute encoder, and a motor driver in a single housing. A cross-roller bearing supports simultaneous axial, radial, and tilting loads transmitted from the ground through the wheel uprights. The actuators produce a continuous torque of 8.3~N$\cdot$m and a peak torque of 24.8~N$\cdot$m under 24~V operation. The integrated 14-bit encoder provides angular position feedback with a resolution of $0.022^\circ$, used for closed-loop position control over a secondary CAN bus network.

Low-level actuator control, safety monitoring, and sensor feedback are executed on a DJI RoboMaster Development Board Type C. The board houses an STM32F407IGH6 microcontroller (ARM Cortex-M4 core running at 168~MHz with hardware floating-point support). Two independent CAN bus controllers interface directly with the four traction ESCs and the four steering actuators. The board also incorporates a 6-axis inertial measurement unit (IMU) mounted on a mechanical vibration-damping structure. Deterministic firmware on the microcontroller executes position and velocity loops at fixed intervals, parses motion commands from the onboard computer, and transmits wheel encoder odometry and IMU telemetry.

Visual sensing is provided by an Intel RealSense D435 camera mounted on the front section of the robot frame. The sensor connects to the onboard computer via a USB 3.2 Gen~1 interface. For the navigation pipeline, the camera operates in RGB streaming mode at a resolution of $640 \times 480$ pixels and a frame rate of 30 frames per second (fps). The stream is captured as JPEG-compressed frames to minimize transfer overhead across the ROS~2 transport layer. The camera is positioned at height $h$ above the ground with a downward tilt angle $\alpha$ toward the inter-row path, providing a field of view that encompasses the corridor surface and adjacent crop boundaries in front of the vehicle.

\subsection{Computing platform}
\label{subsec:computing}

Onboard processing is executed on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin Developer Kit (64~GB version). The computing unit incorporates a 12-core ARM Cortex-A78AE v8.2 64-bit CPU operating at 2.20~GHz and an NVIDIA Ampere architecture GPU with 2048 CUDA cores and 64 Tensor cores. The module delivers up to 275~TOPS of sparse INT8 compute capability and 138~TFLOPS in FP16 Tensor Core operations.

The system shares 64~GB of unified LPDDR5 memory between the CPU and GPU over a 256-bit bus, providing a memory bandwidth of 204.8~GB/s. Persistent storage and dataset logging use a 512~GB NVMe SSD mounted over an M.2 PCIe Gen4 $\times$4 interface at \texttt{/mnt/nvme}, leaving the 64~GB onboard eMMC memory dedicated to the base operating system.

The software stack runs on Ubuntu 22.04.5 LTS with Linux kernel \texttt{5.15.148-\allowbreak tegra} and NVIDIA Linux for Tegra (L4T) release R36.4.4 under JetPack 6.1. The complete robotics and deep learning environment is containerized using Docker and the NVIDIA Container Runtime, providing pass-through access to the GPU, hardware accelerators, and USB character devices. Within the container, ROS~2 Humble Hawksbill coordinates image acquisition, neural network inference with ONNX Runtime 1.23.0 using the \texttt{CUDAExecutionProvider}, geometric line extraction, and low-level serial communication.

The RealSense node (\texttt{/camera/camera}) publishes the compressed color images. The controller node of the package \texttt{seg\_controller\_onnx} subscribes to these images and runs, in the same callback, the segmentation network, the path extractor, the error filter, and the steering law. The image subscription keeps only the last message (\texttt{KEEP\_LAST} with depth 1) with \texttt{BEST\_EFFORT} reliability, so a frame that arrives while the callback is still running replaces the previous one instead of being queued.

\subsubsection{Control node and error conditioning}
\label{subsubsec:control_node}

The control node converts the image-space geometric reference into linear and angular velocity commands $(v, \omega)$ published on the \texttt{/cmd\_vel} topic. Tracking errors extracted from the segmentation mask at reference row $y_{\mathrm{ref}} = 0.50 \cdot H$ ($y = 112\text{ px}$ for a $224 \times 224$ input resolution) are subject to sensor noise and transient occlusions. To ensure smooth closed-loop actuation without erratic steering adjustments, raw lateral pixel errors $e_{\mathrm{px}}$ pass through a multi-stage conditioning filter before entering the steering law.

\paragraph{Dual-regime recursive smoothing and jump rejection.}
The core filter operates as an exponential moving average (EMA) conditioned on the path finder tracking state $\mathrm{status}_{\mathrm{pf}} \in \{\mathrm{ok}, \mathrm{degraded}\}$. The filter smoothing factor $\alpha$ and jump threshold $J_{\mathrm{thr}}$ switch dynamically between two operating regimes:
\begin{equation}
    (\alpha, J_{\mathrm{thr}}) = 
    \begin{cases}
        (0.20, \; 40.0\text{ px}) & \text{if } \mathrm{status}_{\mathrm{pf}} = \mathrm{ok}, \\
        (0.18, \; 38.0\text{ px}) & \text{if } \mathrm{status}_{\mathrm{pf}} = \mathrm{degraded}.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:filter_regimes}
\end{equation}
At each sampled frame $k$, the discrepancy between incoming measurement $e_{\mathrm{px},k}$ and prior filtered state $e_{k-1}$ is evaluated as $\Delta_k = |e_{\mathrm{px},k} - e_{k-1}|$. When $\Delta_k \le J_{\mathrm{thr}}$, the measurement is accepted as nominal and updated via standard EMA:
\begin{equation}
    e_k = (1 - \alpha) e_{k-1} + \alpha \, e_{\mathrm{px},k},
    \label{eq:filter_ema_update}
\end{equation}
with the consecutive jump counter reset to zero ($c_{\mathrm{jump}} = 0$).

Conversely, when $\Delta_k > J_{\mathrm{thr}}$, the counter increments ($c_{\mathrm{jump}} \leftarrow c_{\mathrm{jump}} + 1$). If the anomaly occurs on a single isolated frame ($c_{\mathrm{jump}} = 1$), it is treated as a spurious segmentation outlier and heavily attenuated:
\begin{equation}
    e_k = 0.80 \, e_{k-1} + 0.20 \, e_{\mathrm{px},k}.
    \label{eq:filter_outlier_damp}
\end{equation}
If the large displacement persists across consecutive frames ($c_{\mathrm{jump}} \ge 2$), the filter classifies the variation as a genuine path change, resets $c_{\mathrm{jump}} = 0$, and assigns $e_k = e_{\mathrm{px},k}$ directly to avoid phase lag.

\paragraph{Temporal hold and command deadband.}
When the path finder reports a lost tracking state or when no valid centerline is detected ($e_{\mathrm{px}} = \text{None}$), the filter initiates a zero-order temporal hold:
\begin{equation}
    e_k = e_{\mathrm{last\_valid}} \quad \text{for } \Delta t \le t_{\mathrm{hold}},
    \label{eq:filter_hold}
\end{equation}
where $t_{\mathrm{hold}} = 0.50\text{ s}$ ($\approx 15\text{ frames}$ at $30\text{ fps}$). If perception dropouts exceed $0.50\text{ s}$, the filter clears its internal state and issues an emergency stop command ($v = 0$, $\omega = 0$).

To eliminate high-frequency actuator jitter when the vehicle is aligned with the corridor, an output deadband of $\pm 4.0\text{ px}$ is applied:
\begin{equation}
    e_{\mathrm{cmd},k} = 
    \begin{cases}
        0.0 & \text{if } |e_k| < 4.0\text{ px}, \\
        e_k & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:filter_deadband}
\end{equation}
The deadband alters only command output $e_{\mathrm{cmd},k}$, preserving internal state $e_k$ to avoid filter deadlock when crossing the zero-error threshold.

\paragraph{Velocity command generation.}
The filtered lateral error is normalized relative to the image half-width, $e_{\mathrm{norm}} = e_{\mathrm{cmd}} / (W / 2)$, and scaled by the geometric extraction confidence $C \in [0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
    \omega = - K_{p,\mathrm{lat}} \cdot (0.6 + 0.4 \cdot C) \cdot e_{\mathrm{norm}},
    \label{eq:control_law_omega}
\end{equation}
where $K_{p,\mathrm{lat}} = 1.2$ is the lateral proportional gain. Calculated angular velocity is clamped within $[-\omega_{\max}, \omega_{\max}]$, while forward linear speed is set to $v = v_{\mathrm{nom}}$. \subsection{Control interface}
\label{subsec:control_interface}

The control node translates the guidance reference into velocity commands published as \texttt{geometry\_msgs/msg/Twist} messages on the \texttt{/cmd\_vel} topic. These commands specify the forward linear speed $v$ along the longitudinal chassis axis and the angular velocity $\omega$ about the vertical yaw axis.

A dedicated serial communication node, \texttt{demetra\_serial\_driver}, interfaces the ROS~2 environment with the low-level STM32 microcontroller. The physical connection uses an FTDI USB-to-UART TTL converter operating at a baud rate of 115200~bps with an update loop running at 100~Hz (10~ms period). Each transmitted packet consists of 24 bytes containing six 32-bit floating-point values followed by a checksum for transmission validation.

On the microcontroller, inverse kinematics converts the target $(v, \omega)$ command into individual steering angles and wheel rotation speeds according to the symmetric 4WS model. For a vehicle with wheelbase $L$ and track width $T$, the nominal steering angle $\delta$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \delta = \arctan\left(\frac{L \omega}{2 v}\right),
    \label{eq:4ws_steering_angle}
\end{equation}
with the front axle steering at $+\delta$ and the rear axle steering at $-\delta$. The individual turn radius for the center of each axle is $R = L / (2\tan\delta)$. To account for track width $T$ and reduce wheel scuffing during cornering, individual steering angles for the left and right wheels follow dual-Ackermann geometry:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_{L} = \arctan\left(\frac{L/2}{R - T/2}\right), \qquad
    \delta_{R} = \arctan\left(\frac{L/2}{R + T/2}\right),
    \label{eq:ackermann_4ws}
\end{equation}
with $\delta_{FL} = \delta_L$, $\delta_{FR} = \delta_R$, $\delta_{RL} = -\delta_L$, and $\delta_{RR} = -\delta_R$. The corresponding wheel linear velocities $v_i$ for each wheel $i \in \{FL, FR, RL, RR\}$ are:
\begin{equation}
    v_i = \omega \sqrt{\left(\frac{L}{2}\right)^2 + R_i^2},
    \label{eq:wheel_velocity}
\end{equation}
where $R_i$ is the lateral distance from the ICR to the wheel center plane. Target wheel angular velocities $\omega_{w,i} = v_i / r_w$ (with wheel radius $r_w$) are converted into motor speed setpoints for the C620 ESCs via the 19:1 gear ratio, while the steering angles are tracked by the AK70-10 actuators via the internal 14-bit encoder position loops.

\section{Deployment configuration}
\label{sec:deployment}

\subsection{Model export to ONNX}
\label{subsec:onnx_export}

The deployed LR-ASPP network (the multi-crop binary model \texttt{best\_allsam2.pth}, Section~\ref{subsec:c5_deployed_network}) is exported from PyTorch to ONNX with \texttt{torch.onnx.export}. The export script rebuilds the two-class LR-ASPP, loads the checkpoint weights, and wraps the network so that it returns only the main output tensor, that is, the per-pixel class logits; softmax and argmax are not part of the exported graph. The example input is a float32 tensor of size $1\times3\times224\times224$, with the batch dimension declared dynamic and the spatial size fixed at $224\times224$. The export uses opset 17 with constant folding, and the exported graph is validated with \texttt{onnx.checker.check\_model}. The ONNX file used on the robot, \texttt{lraspp\_224x224\_allsam2.onnx}, was also compared with the PyTorch checkpoint on the same deterministic random input of size $1\times3\times224\times224$: the maximum absolute difference between the logits was $5.6\times10^{-5}$ and the mean absolute difference $4.9\times10^{-6}$, so the two outputs agree within a relative tolerance of $10^{-3}$ and an absolute tolerance of $10^{-4}$. This comparison was run with PyTorch 2.6.0 and ONNX Runtime 1.23.2 on the CPU.

On the robot, camera frames are resized to $224\times224$ before inference, to match this input size. The inference times in Table~\ref{tab:c5_architecture_results} were instead measured at the original image resolution, so they do not describe the onboard model.

\subsection{Software-in-the-Loop pre-validation}
\label{subsec:sitl}

Before deploying the navigation pipeline on the physical robot, the stability of the full closed-loop chain (segmentation, geometric extraction, error conditioning, steering law, and vehicle kinematics) was verified in a lightweight Software-in-the-Loop (SITL) environment running on a desktop workstation.

\subsubsection{SITL architecture}
\label{subsubsec:sitl_architecture}

The SITL node loads a single real RGB frame acquired by the Intel RealSense D435 camera on an actual crop row. Rather than rendering a synthetic 3D scene, the simulator models the robot's lateral displacement and heading variation as a horizontal translation of the camera viewport over the original image. The frame is padded with reflective borders (400~pixels per side) to allow displacements of up to $\pm 0.45\text{ m}$ without introducing black edges.

The pixel shift $\Delta x$ applied at each simulation step combines two terms derived from the D435 optical parameters:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta x = -y \cdot \frac{f_x}{D} + \theta \cdot f_x,
    \label{eq:sitl_projection}
\end{equation}
where $y$ is the lateral offset of the robot from the corridor center (in meters), $\theta$ is the heading angle relative to the row axis (in radians), $f_x = 465\text{ px}$ is the horizontal focal length of the D435 at $640 \times 480$ resolution, and $D = 1.25\text{ m}$ is the lookahead distance corresponding to the reference evaluation row ($y_{\mathrm{ref}} = 0.50 \cdot H$).

\subsubsection{Kinematic model}
\label{subsubsec:sitl_kinematics}

The SITL integrates the same 4WS symmetric contra-steering kinematics implemented in the onboard firmware (\texttt{kinematics.cpp}), with identical parameters: wheelbase $L = 0.40\text{ m}$, wheel diameter $d_w = 0.192\text{ m}$, and mechanical steering saturation at $\pm 30.0^\circ$. At each time step $\Delta t = 1/30\text{ s}$, the node receives $(v, \omega)$ from the \texttt{/cmd\_vel} topic published by the real \texttt{controller\_node}, computes the effective steering angle:
\begin{equation}
    \delta = \arctan\!\left(\frac{L \cdot \omega}{2\,v}\right), \qquad
    \omega_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{2\,v\,\tan\delta}{L},
    \label{eq:sitl_steering}
\end{equation}
and updates the planar state:
\begin{align}
    \theta_{k+1} &= \theta_k + \omega_{\mathrm{eff}} \cdot \Delta t, \\
    y_{k+1} &= y_k - v \sin\theta_k \cdot \Delta t.
    \label{eq:sitl_integration}
\end{align}
The translated frame is then published on the same ROS~2 topic used by the physical camera (\texttt{/camera/camera/color/image\_raw/compressed}), so the \texttt{controller\_node} processes it without any code modification.

\subsubsection{Interactive perturbation testing}
\label{subsubsec:sitl_perturbation}

Keyboard inputs allow the operator to inject controlled lateral perturbations ($\pm 4\text{ cm}$ or $\pm 10\text{ cm}$) during execution, simulating sudden displacement events such as wheel slip on soft soil or an external push. This procedure was used to verify that the error conditioning filter and steering controller recover to the corridor center within approximately $10\text{--}15$ frames ($\sim 0.5\text{ s}$ at $30\text{ fps}$), without residual oscillations and without saturating the $\pm 30^\circ$ mechanical steering limit. Table~\ref{tab:controller_parameters} lists the filter and control parameters validated through these SITL tests before field deployment.

\chapter{Robot Platform and Integration}
\label{cap:platform}

This chapter describes the robot used in the field tests, the software that runs on it, and the deployment of the segmentation network.

\section{Robot hardware and sensors}
\label{sec:hardware}

\subsection{Robot platform}
\label{subsec:robot_platform}

The experimental platform is Demetra, a mobile robot prototype developed by the Roboto student team of the Politecnico di Torino. The chassis features a four-wheel steering (4WS) and four-wheel drive (4WD) kinematic configuration.

Each wheel has a DJI RoboMaster M3508 brushless motor with a 19:1 planetary gearbox and a DJI RoboMaster C620 speed controller, and is steered by a CubeMars AK70-10 actuator with an integrated encoder. A DJI RoboMaster Development Board Type C (STM32F407) controls the speed controllers and the steering actuators over two CAN buses. It receives the linear velocity and the steering angle from the onboard computer and sends back the measured platform speed and steering angle.

Steering is described with a kinematic bicycle model with symmetric opposite-phase steering: the equivalent front and rear steering angles are $\delta_f=\delta$ and $\delta_r=-\delta$. Assuming pure rolling without lateral slip, the instantaneous center of rotation (ICR) lies on the transverse axis through the midpoint between the two axles. With wheelbase $L$, the signed turning radius of this midpoint is
\begin{equation}
    R = \frac{L}{2 \tan\delta}.
    \label{eq:turning_radius_4ws}
\end{equation}
The wheelbase is $L = 0.40$~m and the wheel diameter is 0.192~m ($r_w = 0.096$~m).

The camera is an Intel RealSense D435, mounted on the front of the robot frame. The color stream is used at $640 \times 480$ pixels and 30 frames per second. The camera is tilted downward, so that the image shows the corridor and the crop rows on both sides in front of the robot.

\subsection{Computing platform}
\label{subsec:computing}

The onboard computer is an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin Developer Kit (64~GB), with a 12-core ARM Cortex-A78AE CPU and an NVIDIA Ampere GPU with 2048 CUDA cores. It runs Ubuntu 22.04 with L4T R36.4.4. The software runs in a Docker container with access to the GPU and to the USB devices. Inside the container, ROS~2 Humble runs the camera driver, the controller node, and the serial driver.

\section{Software architecture}
\label{sec:software}

\subsection{ROS integration}
\label{subsec:ros}

The software pipeline is implemented in ROS~2. Figure~\ref{fig:ros2_architecture} shows the node graph recorded on the robot during navigation. Three nodes are active. The RealSense node (\texttt{/camera/camera}) publishes the compressed color images. The controller node (\texttt{/controller\_node}) subscribes to these images. In the same callback, it runs the segmentation network and the path extractor, computes the velocity command (Section~\ref{subsubsec:control_node}), and publishes it on \texttt{/cmd\_vel}. The serial driver node (\texttt{/demetra\_node}) receives the commands and sends them to the microcontroller. The controller node also publishes a text log on \texttt{/debug/text}. When the debug video is enabled, it publishes three debug images on other \texttt{/debug} topics. The debug video is used only during development and is disabled when the robot operates. The \texttt{/debug} topics are omitted from the figure.

The image subscription keeps only the last frame, so frames that arrive during the processing are not queued.

\subsection{Controller node}
\label{subsubsec:control_node}

For each frame, the controller node decodes the image, resizes it to $224\times224$ pixels and normalizes it with the ImageNet mean and standard deviation. The segmentation network turns the image into the label mask, and the path extractor (Chapter~\ref{cap:geometric_reference}) turns the mask into a centerline.

The lateral error $e_{\mathrm{px}}$ is the horizontal offset, in pixels, of the centerline from the image center at row $y_{\mathrm{ref}} = 156$ of the $W \times H = 224\times224$ input (70\% of $H$, truncated). It is positive when the centerline is on the right. Before the steering law, the error passes through a filter that depends on the status returned by the path extractor.

\paragraph{Exponential filter and jump rejection.}
With status \texttt{ok} or \texttt{degraded}, the filtered error $e_k$ is updated with an exponential moving average (EMA). The reference smoothing factor $\alpha_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and the jump threshold $J$ depend on the status:
\begin{equation}
    (\alpha_{\mathrm{ref}}, J) =
    \begin{cases}
        (0.20, \; 40\text{ px}) & \text{if the status is \texttt{ok}}, \\
        (0.18, \; 38\text{ px}) & \text{if the status is \texttt{degraded}}.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:filter_regimes}
\end{equation}
The factor $\alpha$ used in the update is obtained from $\alpha_{\mathrm{ref}}$ with Equation~\eqref{eq:alpha_dt}. With $\Delta_k = |e_{\mathrm{px},k} - e_{k-1}|$, the measurement is accepted when $\Delta_k \le J$:
\begin{equation}
    e_k = (1 - \alpha)\, e_{k-1} + \alpha \, e_{\mathrm{px},k}.
    \label{eq:filter_ema_update}
\end{equation}
When $\Delta_k > J$ on an isolated frame, the update uses a fixed weight of 0.20, $e_k = 0.80\, e_{k-1} + 0.20\, e_{\mathrm{px},k}$. When the jump is present in two consecutive frames, the filter takes the new measurement directly, $e_k = e_{\mathrm{px},k}$.

\paragraph{Smoothing independent of the frame rate.}
The reference factors are defined for 30 frames per second. At each frame, the node measures the time $\Delta t$ since the previous frame, limited to the interval from $1/120$ to $0.25$~s, and converts each reference factor $\alpha_{\mathrm{ref}}$ into
\begin{equation}
    \alpha = 1 - (1 - \alpha_{\mathrm{ref}})^{30\,\Delta t}.
    \label{eq:alpha_dt}
\end{equation}
With $\Delta t = 1/30$~s, $\alpha = \alpha_{\mathrm{ref}}$. When frames arrive less often, $\alpha$ grows, so the time constant of the filter in seconds does not change with the processing rate.

\paragraph{Hold, stop, and deadband.}
When the status is \texttt{lost} or no centerline is available, the node keeps the last filtered error for up to $t_{\mathrm{hold}} = 0.5$~s. After this time it publishes a zero velocity command. After a stop, the error filter restarts from the first new measurement and the filter of the angular velocity, described below, from zero. A filtered error below 4~px in absolute value is set to zero in the command, while the filter keeps its internal value.

\paragraph{Steering law.}
The command error $e_{\mathrm{cmd}}$ is $e_k$ after the deadband. It is normalized by the half-width of the image and scaled by the confidence $C \in [0, 1]$ of the path extractor:
\begin{equation}
    \omega^{*} = - K_{p} \, (0.6 + 0.4\, C) \, \frac{e_{\mathrm{cmd}}}{W/2},
    \label{eq:control_law_omega}
\end{equation}
with $K_{p} = 1.2$. The value is limited to $\pm\omega_{\max} = \pm 0.58$~rad/s. With Equation~\eqref{eq:4ws_steering_angle}, this limit corresponds to $\delta \approx 30^\circ$ at $v = 0.20$~m/s. A second EMA smooths the angular velocity sent to the robot, $\omega_k = (1 - \alpha_w)\,\omega_{k-1} + \alpha_w\,\omega^{*}$, with $\alpha_w = 0.30$ at 30 frames per second, converted with Equation~\eqref{eq:alpha_dt}. The linear velocity is constant, $v = 0.20$~m/s. Table~\ref{tab:controller_parameters} lists the parameters used on the robot.

\subsection{Control interface}
\label{subsec:control_interface}

The serial driver node (\texttt{/demetra\_node}) converts each command $(v, \omega)$ into the steering angle
\begin{equation}
    \delta = \arctan\left(\frac{L\, \omega}{2\, v}\right),
    \label{eq:4ws_steering_angle}
\end{equation}
limited to $\pm 30^\circ$. Before the conversion, the node limits $v$ to $\pm 1.5$~m/s and $\omega$ to $\pm 6.0$~rad/s. Every 10~ms it sends the last $v$ and $\delta$ to the microcontroller over a serial link at 115200~baud. If no command arrives for 500~ms, it sets the speed to zero.

\begingroup\color{red}
The microcontroller computes the steering angle of each wheel and the wheel speeds. For track width $T$ and turning radius $R = L / (2\tan\delta)$, the left and right steering angles follow the dual-Ackermann geometry:
\begin{equation}
    \delta_{L} = \arctan\left(\frac{L/2}{R - T/2}\right), \qquad
    \delta_{R} = \arctan\left(\frac{L/2}{R + T/2}\right),
    \label{eq:ackermann_4ws}
\end{equation}
with $\delta_{FL} = \delta_L$, $\delta_{FR} = \delta_R$, $\delta_{RL} = -\delta_L$, and $\delta_{RR} = -\delta_R$. The linear velocity of each wheel $i \in \{FL, FR, RL, RR\}$ is
\begin{equation}
    v_i = \omega \sqrt{\left(\frac{L}{2}\right)^2 + R_i^2},
    \label{eq:wheel_velocity}
\end{equation}
where $R_i$ is the lateral distance from the ICR to the wheel center plane. The wheel speeds $v_i / r_w$ are converted into motor speed setpoints through the 19:1 gear ratio.
\endgroup

\subsection{Timing and latency}
\label{subsec:timing}

The deployed LR-ASPP network (Section~\ref{subsec:c5_deployed_network}) is exported from PyTorch to ONNX and run with ONNX Runtime. The inference times in Table~\ref{tab:c5_architecture_results} were measured on the RTX 2080 SUPER used for training, at the original image resolution, so they do not describe the onboard model.

The processing times were measured on the Jetson AGX Orin by passing 100 compressed camera frames, recorded in the \texttt{insalata\_grande1} folder, through the callback of the controller node, after 5 warm-up frames. For each frame, the node records the inference time, the time of the path extractor (\texttt{pf4.py}, an earlier version of Strip-IRLS that selected the seed by edge support and used 24 strips), and the total time of the callback. The first 50 frames were processed with the debug video enabled, which renders and compresses the debug images, and the other 50 with the debug video disabled. Table~\ref{tab:jetson_timing} reports the mean and standard deviation of each time.

\subsection{Software-in-the-Loop test}
\label{subsec:sitl}

Before the field tests, the complete pipeline was tested in closed loop on a desktop computer, without moving the robot. A simulation node shifts a recorded camera frame horizontally according to the lateral offset and the heading of a simulated robot, which it updates from the velocity commands with the kinematic model of Section~\ref{subsec:robot_platform}. The controller node runs on these frames without changes.


\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{cap:conclusions}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Our solution is a camera-based guidance chain for a robot between crop rows: a segmentation network trained on labels generated by SAM~3, an extractor that turns the mask into a reference line, and a proportional controller, all running on the Jetson AGX Orin of the robot.

Among the three architectures, LR-ASPP was chosen for the robot. Its mIoU is 1.08 percentage points lower than that of FCN and DeepLabV3, but it is 7 to 11 times faster, and for a network that runs on the robot in real time, speed is the most important requirement.

Automatic labels can replace manual annotation for the path, but not for every class. Networks trained only on uncorrected SAM~3 labels reach a path IoU of about 0.90 between parallel rows, about 6 percentage points below a network trained on manual labels. SAM~3 can label some classes or scenes poorly, such as the covering material, and it also labels as path the ground outside the corridor, so whether automatic labels are suitable depends on the task. For the path between parallel rows, the class used here, they were enough, as the field tests show. The label configuration has almost no effect on the path between parallel rows: there the path IoU of the four-class, three-class and binary networks differs by less than 0.5 percentage points. The binary configuration was adopted because the extractors use only the path.

Our two extractors, Strip-IRLS and EdgeNet, were compared with three baselines. On masks with one corridor and on the annotated real frames, they and RANSAC edges have few gross errors. Center points has slightly more on the synthetic masks with one corridor, and on the annotated frames the four differ by a few frames, so these tests do not rank them. With adjacent corridors RANSAC edges has many gross errors, while Strip-IRLS, EdgeNet and Center points have few. Column histogram has many gross errors in every group. On the Jetson, Column histogram takes 0.91~ms, EdgeNet 4.68~ms, Center points 7.27~ms, Strip-IRLS 11.07~ms and RANSAC edges 15.52~ms. EdgeNet, trained only on synthetic masks, performs like Strip-IRLS, RANSAC edges and Center points. Since it works on the mask, its training data are generated with the exact centerline. With EdgeNet, both the segmentation and the extraction of the centerline are therefore learned from labels made without manual annotation, the SAM~3 labels and the generated masks. Since it has no geometric parameters to set by hand, it can replace the hand tuning of geometric thresholds.

On the Jetson, without the debug video, the whole callback takes 11.3 to 26.2~ms depending on the extractor, less than the 33.3~ms frame period of the camera, so every frame is processed. In the field, the robot followed a tomato row not present in the training data of the network, a difficult case with sparse plants and poor light, with each of the five extractors and with only the extractor changing between runs. Its distance from the reference centerline stayed within 13.6~cm on the short runs and 20.4~cm on the full row, so a network trained only on automatic labels was enough to guide the robot along the row. Strip-IRLS, EdgeNet, RANSAC edges and Center points drove it along almost the same trajectory, and along the full row Column histogram followed it too, up to 10~cm away. On the annotated real frames, Strip-IRLS, EdgeNet and RANSAC edges fail mostly on the same badly segmented frames. The tests on the annotated frames and in the field therefore show that the quality of the segmentation affects the result more than the choice among these extractors.

\section{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:limitations_future}

The segmentation was measured against manual masks only on lettuce, with a random split by image, so training and test images are different frames of the same paths and the results can be optimistic.

The offline comparison of the extractors has two limits. The baselines are implementations of this work and differ from the original methods. \textit{Column histogram}, for example, is a simplified version of SegMin (Section~\ref{subsec:c5_histogram_baseline}). Their results may therefore not hold for the published methods, and a comparison with the original implementations, where available, would show the difference. The test data are also limited. The synthetic masks are straight corridors whose edges have irregularities of a few pixels, while the real predictions often have rounded boundaries. Most of the 255 annotated frames come from paths used to train the network, although the frames themselves were not used in training, so their masks are probably cleaner than on new paths. These frames are not independent and are too few to rank the best extractors. More frames from new recordings would resolve their differences.

The extractors themselves have three limits. Strip-IRLS and Center points grow the corridor from its bottom strips, so a corridor whose bottom is empty, split or part of a full-width band of path can make them fail. A global method that uses the whole mask, such as \textit{EdgeNet}, does not need a seed and could handle these cases. The confidence scores are heuristic and do not always follow the error. EdgeNet returns \texttt{lost} on 25 synthetic masks with adjacent corridors where its line is correct (Section~\ref{subsubsec:geometric_comparison}), while all the gross errors of Strip-IRLS and RANSAC edges on the annotated frames are classified as \texttt{ok} (Section~\ref{subsubsec:geometric_annotated}). A confidence calibrated against the error on annotated real frames could keep the correct lines and return \texttt{lost} on the wrong ones. Finally, the geometric extractors need their parameters to be set by hand. Strip-IRLS has 16 arguments and 13 constants (Table~\ref{tab:c5_defaults}), several of them in pixels of the mask, and they were set for the camera and mounting of this robot. EdgeNet has no geometric parameters to set, because it learns from its generator. Tests with a different camera or mounting would show whether the results of the geometric extractors degrade more than those of EdgeNet. These parameters are also fixed, and they could be made to change while the robot moves. EdgeNet is still at an early stage, but its results are promising and it should be explored further.

The field tests were made in one row of one crop on one day, and the reference centerline was placed by eye at five points. Tests in more rows, crops and light conditions, with a reference measured at more points, would separate better the error of the reference from that of the robot. A further step is to let the robot navigate the whole greenhouse, whatever the layout of its rows, and not only along one row, for example by moving from one row to the next.

When the reference is lost for more than 0.5~s the robot stops, and a wrong segmentation that persists over consecutive frames can steer it towards the plants. Handling both cases better is left to future work. The chain also has no explicit detection of obstacles on the path, and an obstacle avoidance module could be added to it.

\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{cap:conclusions}

This chapter summarizes the main findings, discusses the contributions and limitations of the work, and identifies directions for future research.

\section{Main contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This thesis investigated vision-based local guidance for agricultural row navigation through segmentation model selection, dataset preparation with automatic SAM~3 annotation, geometric reference extraction, and the integration and test of these stages on a robot. The main contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Architecture comparison for robotic segmentation:} FCN, DeepLabV3, and LR-ASPP were compared on manually annotated lettuce images. LR-ASPP was selected for its accuracy--speed compromise: on the test images it reached a path IoU of 0.9532 with a recorded inference time of 8.21~ms, against 59.10~ms and 86.90~ms for the other architectures, at the cost of an mIoU about 1.1 percentage points lower.

\item \textbf{Automatic dataset annotation with SAM~3:} Training labels were generated with SAM~3 text prompts and used without any manual correction. Networks trained on these labels for lettuce were compared with a network trained on manual masks, and the binary labeling was then extended to the complete lettuce, tomato, and cucumber collection to train the network deployed on the robot.

\item \textbf{Evaluation of output label configurations:} Networks with four-class, three-class, and binary path outputs, trained on SAM~3 lettuce labels, were compared against the manual ground truth. Their path IoU values differ by less than 0.5 percentage points in the realistic scenario. The binary configuration was adopted for the robot because the path is the only class used by the geometric extraction.

\item \textbf{Edge-based geometric extractor and its evaluation:} An extractor that converts corridor masks into image-space navigation references was implemented (Strip-IRLS). It uses horizontal strip extraction, bottom-up interval association with selection of the chain that gives the most edge samples, exclusion of the edge samples clipped by the image border, and separate fits of the left and right edges with iteratively reweighted least squares. It was compared with three baseline extractors (Center points, Column histogram, RANSAC edges) on synthetic masks with exact ground truth and on real frames of three crops with manually annotated corridor edges.

\item \textbf{Edge regression network, a first experiment:} A small network that takes the binary mask and regresses the two corridor edges was designed and trained on synthetic masks. It has the same interface as the geometric extractors and was compared with them in the same tests. It is at an early stage.

\item \textbf{Integration on the robot and field tests:} The segmentation network, the five extractors and a controller with an exponential moving average filter, a rejection of isolated jumps, a deadband and a proportional steering law run in one ROS~2 node on the Jetson AGX Orin of the robot (Chapter~\ref{cap:platform}). Without the debug video, the callback takes 11.3 to 26.2~ms depending on the extractor, less than the 33.3~ms frame period of the camera. All five extractors were tested in closed loop in a tomato row, with the trajectory of the robot measured by a total station (Section~\ref{sec:field_validation}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions on segmentation and annotation}
\label{sec:segmentation_conclusions}

The architecture comparison supports LR-ASPP as a compromise between segmentation accuracy and inference time, measured on the training GPU. Whether its segmentation is sufficient for navigation is assessed through the geometric extraction and the robot tests, not from IoU alone.

On lettuce, the manual model remains the best: path IoU 0.9532 on the complete test set and 0.9587 in the realistic scenario. Networks trained only on uncorrected SAM~3 labels reach a path IoU of about 0.90 in the realistic scenario (0.9013 for the binary network), but only 0.39--0.41 on the complete test set, and the 4-class network reaches an mIoU of 0.5925. Fully automatic labels therefore provide path supervision of about 0.90 IoU in the operational conditions, but they are not a replacement for manual annotation when all classes are needed or when the scene departs from those conditions. The multi-crop binary configuration used on the robot, trained on the complete SAM~3 collection without any manual annotation, reaches 0.9071 in the realistic scenario and 0.6369 on the complete test set when the lettuce test images are excluded from its training data. The deployed weights were assessed visually, in simulation, and on the robot.

The choice of output label configuration affects both annotation effort and downstream geometric extraction. Binary path segmentation focuses the model directly on the navigation target, and it makes both the labeling task and the geometric stage simpler. This comes at the cost of discarding plant and covering-material information that might be useful for other tasks.

\section{Conclusions on path extraction}
\label{sec:geometric_conclusions}

The geometric extraction stage converts binary corridor segmentation masks into navigation setpoints. The offline comparison of Chapter~\ref{cap:experiments} gives the following results:
\begin{itemize}
    \item On real frames with manually annotated edges, Strip-IRLS and RANSAC edges have the fewest failures, with 2.3\% and 2.3\% gross errors on the development frames and 2.5\% and 0.8\% on the test frames. The difference is a few frames and the order changes between the two parts. The network has 3.0\% and 6.6\%, Center points and Column histogram 5.5 to 18.8\%.
    \item The synthetic masks show a different failure case for each extractor: for Strip-IRLS a hole wider than its merging gap and taller than its seed window, and a narrow row between adjacent corridors, for RANSAC edges adjacent corridors, for Center points disturbances on one side, for Column histogram an offset or rotated robot, and for EdgeNet a bulge on one side of the corridor, where it has its only two gross errors in the base scenarios.
    \item Variants of Strip-IRLS tuned on the synthetic development masks were not better on the real annotated frames. Synthetic benchmarks written by the same author as the extractors are useful to find failure cases, but the choice between versions needs real annotated data.
    \item The parameters of Strip-IRLS were set by hand for the camera, its mounting and the fields of this work, and in a new scenario they may need to be adjusted to get the best results. EdgeNet needs no geometric parameters, so adapting it means changing its generator instead of tuning thresholds. With one model for three crops it is close to the geometric extractors but not better, and its accuracy depends on how well the generator covers the field masks.
    \item For Strip-IRLS, RANSAC edges and EdgeNet the status is a weak indicator of a correct line, because most of their gross errors are classified as \texttt{ok}.
\end{itemize}

No extractor is best on every test set.

\section{Conclusions on the field tests}
\label{sec:field_conclusions}

The five extractors were tested in closed loop in a row of tomato plants. The plants were sparse, so the predicted path often widened into the gaps between them, and the light was poor. No image of this field is in the training data of the segmentation network (Section~\ref{subsec:test_conditions}).

Strip-IRLS, EdgeNet, RANSAC edges and Center points give almost the same trajectory. On the short runs their mean lateral distance from the row centerline is between $+2.9$ and $+4.2$~cm, and the largest absolute value between 10.1 and 11.0~cm. On the full row the mean is between $-6.2$ and $-7.2$~cm and the largest absolute value between 16.3 and 20.4~cm. The root mean square difference of each of them from the mean of the other extractors is 1.1 to 2.1~cm, while the lateral distance they have in common varies by about 24~cm along the full row. These runs therefore do not rank the four extractors. The common part repeats at the same positions of the row, so it comes from the masks predicted in this field or from the reference, which was placed by eye at five points. The measurements cannot separate the two causes.

Column histogram is the only extractor whose runs differ from the others and from each other, with a root mean square difference of 6.8~cm on the short runs and 4.1~cm on the full row. It classified as \texttt{ok} also the frames in which its centerline was at the image border.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The main limitations of the work are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Structural limits of Strip-IRLS:} The dependence on the seed in the bottom strips, the number of parameters and the effect of missing plants on one side are described in Section~\ref{sec:c5_tradeoffs_and_limitations}. On the synthetic masks the seed and the merging gap cause the gross errors in \texttt{hole\_bottom\_tall} and \texttt{adjacent+offset} (Section~\ref{subsubsec:geometric_comparison}).

\item \textbf{Geometric model constraints:} Strip-IRLS, RANSAC edges, Center points and the edge network model the corridor with straight lines. They cannot follow curved crop rows or navigate headland turnaround maneuvers where crop boundaries terminate or diverge.

\item \textbf{Dataset annotation coverage:} Segmentation accuracy was measured against manual ground truth only on lettuce. The deployed weights have no independent IoU, because their training data include the lettuce test images. The multi-crop configuration was evaluated through a separate network trained without them. Tomato and cucumber have no manual ground truth. The random image-level split also takes training and test images from the same lettuce folders, each recorded along one path. No test image is used for training, but the reported IoU values may be optimistic for paths not seen during training.

\item \textbf{Label definition mismatch:} The manual masks mark only the central traversable corridor as path, whereas networks trained on SAM~3 labels can also segment adjacent or non-traversable corridors. This lowers their IoU against the manual masks, but the size of the effect was not measured.

\item \textbf{Quality of the SAM~3 labels:} Visual inspection showed that SAM~3 segmented the plastic mulch film poorly and also assigned the greenhouse plastic cover to the covering class, and that its path masks were less accurate in images taken from unusual viewpoints or where the path between the rows is not clearly visible. Because the labels were used without correction, these errors enter the training data, and their extent was not measured.

\item \textbf{Annotation effort measurement:} The time saved by automatic labeling with respect to manual annotation was not measured.

\item \textbf{Offline test data:} The synthetic masks are straight corridors whose edges have irregularities of a few pixels, while the real predictions often have rounded boundaries. Only 255 real frames have an annotated ground truth, marked once by one person, and the frames skipped or with a nearly horizontal edge, which are among the most difficult, are excluded. The annotated frames of the SAM~3 dataset come from images used to train the network, so their masks are probably cleaner than predictions on new images.

\item \textbf{Parameters of the geometric extractors:} The parameters of Strip-IRLS and of the baselines were chosen for the camera, its mounting and the fields of this work (Section~\ref{sec:c5_parameter_setting}). With another camera, mounting or crop, they may need to be adjusted to get the best results.

\item \textbf{Confidence of the extractors:} The confidence scores are heuristic and not calibrated, and most gross errors of Strip-IRLS, RANSAC edges and the edge network are classified as \texttt{ok}.

\item \textbf{Field tests:} The tests were made in one row of one crop on one day, with three short runs of 8~m and one run along the full row per extractor. The reference of the row center was placed by eye at five points, so the measured lateral distance also contains its error. Attempts that did not go well were repeated and only the repeated runs are reported, so the tests do not give a failure rate.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:future_work}

Several directions for future research emerge from this work:

\subsection{Dataset and annotation}
\label{subsec:future_annotation}

\begin{itemize}
\item Measure the annotation time of manual and automatic workflows, and test whether a light correction of the SAM~3 labels improves the trained networks.
\item Split the data by folder rather than by image, so that the test paths are not represented in training, and repeat the evaluation of all networks.
\item Prepare manual ground-truth masks for tomato and cucumber to evaluate the deployed network on those crops directly.
\item Add images of fields like the one of the field tests, with sparse plants and poor light, to the training data.
\item Investigate active learning strategies to select images for manual annotation that improve model generalization most effectively.
\item Explore other foundation models or fine-tuned SAM variants specialized for agricultural segmentation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Segmentation and model deployment}
\label{subsec:future_segmentation}

\begin{itemize}
\item Evaluate additional lightweight architectures (e.g., BiSeNet, FastSCNN) for further latency reduction.
\item Investigate quantization and model compression to reduce the inference time on the Jetson AGX Orin further.
\item Test temporal consistency methods that use previous frames to improve segmentation and reduce flickering.
\item Explore uncertainty estimation to detect when segmentation confidence is low and fallback strategies are needed.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Geometric extraction}
\label{subsec:future_geometric}

\begin{itemize}
\item Extend the geometric representation from linear models to piecewise linear or polynomial formulations to support curved rows and headland turns.
\item Implement top-down recovery logic to maintain guidance when bottom-up seeding fails, and handle a corridor that rises from a full-width band of path.
\item Annotate more real frames, from new recordings and by more than one annotator, so that the differences of a few frames between the best extractors can be resolved and the annotation error can be measured.
\item Calibrate the confidence of the extractors, or add a temporal consistency check, so that wrong lines can be rejected before they reach the controller.
\item Extend the generator of the edge network to other cameras, mountings and crops, so that a new scenario needs a new training set and not a new tuning of geometric thresholds.
\item Evaluate the edge network on annotated frames of new recordings, and calibrate its status thresholds for its own confidence.
\item Adapt spatial gate thresholds dynamically using vehicle attitude telemetry from an onboard IMU to prevent false tracking loss during pitch and roll variations.
\item Incorporate temporal Bayesian tracking (such as an Extended Kalman Filter) to smooth reference parameters across frame dropouts.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{System integration and field validation}
\label{subsec:future_integration}

\begin{itemize}
\item Repeat the field tests in more rows, crops, growth stages and lighting conditions, with longer runs and a reference measured at more points along the row, so that the error of the reference can be separated from that of the robot and a failure rate can be measured.
\item Predict the reference from the motion of the robot, so that guidance continues during reference losses longer than the 0.5~s hold of the controller.
\item Investigate fusion with other sensors (e.g., depth, IMU) to keep a valid reference when the camera view is degraded.
\item Develop a complete autonomy system including global path planning, obstacle avoidance, and task-specific behaviors.
\end{itemize}

\section{Closing remarks}
\label{sec:closing}

This thesis addressed the segmentation, dataset preparation, geometric extraction and on-robot integration stages of vision-based agricultural navigation. The selection of LR-ASPP balances accuracy and speed for robotic deployment. Labels generated by SAM~3 without manual correction were used to train the network deployed on the robot, and the geometric extractor converts masks into navigation references using horizontal strip selection and independent edge fitting. In the field tests, the trajectories of four of the five extractors differ by 1.1 to 2.1~cm (root mean square) from the mean of the others.

Future research should extend the geometric model to handle curves, evaluate the deployed network on independent, sequence-level test data, and repeat the field tests in more fields with a more accurate reference.

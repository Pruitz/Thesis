
\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{cap:conclusions}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Our solution is a camera-based guidance chain for a robot between crop rows: a segmentation network trained on labels generated by SAM~3, an extractor that turns the mask into a reference line, and a proportional controller, all running on the Jetson AGX Orin of the robot.

Automatic labels can replace manual annotation for the path, but not for every class. Networks trained only on uncorrected SAM~3 labels reach a path IoU of about 0.90 between parallel rows, about 6 percentage points below a network trained on manual labels. SAM~3 can label some classes or scenes poorly, such as the covering material, and it also labels as path the ground outside the corridor, so whether automatic labels are suitable depends on the task. For the path between parallel rows, the class used here, they were enough, as the field tests show.

Among the three architectures, LR-ASPP was chosen for the robot. Its mIoU is 1.08 percentage points lower than that of FCN and DeepLabV3, but it is 7 to 11 times faster, and for a network that runs on the robot in real time, speed is the most important requirement. The label configuration has almost no effect on the path between parallel rows: there the path IoU of the four-class, three-class and binary networks differs by less than 0.5 percentage points. The binary configuration was adopted because the extractors use only the path.

Our two extractors, Strip-IRLS and EdgeNet, were compared with three baselines. On masks with one corridor and on the annotated real frames, they and RANSAC edges have few gross errors, and these tests do not rank the three. With adjacent corridors RANSAC edges has many gross errors, while Strip-IRLS and EdgeNet have few. Center points loses many more frames, and Column histogram has the most gross errors. On the Jetson, Strip-IRLS is 1.4 times and EdgeNet 3.3 times faster than RANSAC edges. EdgeNet, trained only on synthetic masks, performs like the geometric extractors. Since it has no geometric parameters to set by hand, it can replace the hand tuning of geometric thresholds.

On the Jetson, without the debug video, the whole callback takes 11.3 to 26.2~ms depending on the extractor, less than the 33.3~ms frame period of the camera, so every frame is processed. In the field, the robot followed a tomato row of a field not present in the training data of the network, a difficult case with sparse plants and poor light, with each of the five extractors and with only the extractor changing between runs. Its distance from the reference centerline stayed within 13.6~cm on the short runs and 20.4~cm on the full row, so a network trained only on automatic labels was enough to guide the robot along the row. Strip-IRLS, EdgeNet, RANSAC edges and Center points drove it along almost the same trajectory, and the best extractors fail mostly on the same badly segmented frames. The quality of the segmentation therefore affects the result more than the choice among these extractors.

\section{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:limitations_future}

The segmentation was measured against manual masks only on lettuce, with a random split by image, so training and test images are different frames of the same paths and the results can be optimistic. Manual masks of tomato and cucumber and a split by path would give an independent measure of the deployed network.

The offline comparison of the extractors has two limits. The baselines are implementations of this work and differ from the original methods. \textit{Column histogram}, for example, is a simplified version of SegMin (Section~\ref{sec:c5_baseline_algorithms}). Their results may therefore not hold for the published methods, and a comparison with the original implementations, where available, would show the difference. The test data are also limited. The synthetic masks are straight corridors whose edges have irregularities of a few pixels, while the real predictions often have rounded boundaries. Most of the 255 annotated frames come from paths used to train the network, so their masks are probably cleaner than on new paths, and these frames, which are not independent, are too few to rank the best extractors. More frames from new recordings, marked by more than one annotator, would resolve their differences and measure the annotation error.

The extractors themselves have three limits. Strip-IRLS grows the corridor from its bottom strips (Section~\ref{sec:c5_tradeoffs_and_limitations}), so a corridor whose bottom is empty, split or part of a full-width band of path can make it fail. A global method that uses the whole mask, such as \textit{EdgeNet}, does not need a seed and could handle these cases. The confidence scores are heuristic and do not always follow the error. EdgeNet returns \texttt{lost} on 25 synthetic masks with adjacent corridors where its line is correct (Section~\ref{subsubsec:geometric_comparison}), while all the gross errors of Strip-IRLS and RANSAC edges on the annotated frames are classified as \texttt{ok} (Section~\ref{subsubsec:geometric_annotated}). A confidence calibrated against the error on annotated real frames could keep the correct lines and return \texttt{lost} on the wrong ones. Finally, the thresholds of the geometric extractors were tuned by hand for the camera and mounting of this robot, while EdgeNet learns from its generator. Tests with a different camera or mounting would show whether the results of the geometric extractors degrade more than those of EdgeNet. These thresholds are also fixed, and they could be made to change while the robot moves, for example with its pitch and roll measured by an IMU.

The field tests were made in one row of one crop on one day, and the reference centerline was placed by eye at five points. Tests in more rows, crops and light conditions, with a reference measured at more points, would separate better the error of the reference from that of the robot. When the reference is lost for more than 0.5~s the robot stops, and a wrong segmentation that persists over consecutive frames can steer it towards the plants. Handling both cases better is left to future work. The chain also does not detect obstacles on the path. They could be detected in the depth image of the RealSense D435, which this work does not use.

\section{Context and motivation}
\label{sec:context}

Global population growth and rising food demand exert increasing pressure on agricultural productivity, while the sector faces persistent shortages of skilled labor and the rising cost of farm management~\cite{bai2023vision,silva2023rowdetection}. Addressing these demands requires higher automation in field operations such as weeding, spraying, and crop monitoring. Row-based crops are among the most studied applications of agricultural robots and account for about 75\% of the planted cropland in the United States~\cite{navone2025gpsfree}. Autonomous ground vehicles designed for these settings must navigate along inter-row corridors reliably without damaging crops or compacting productive soil beds.

Outdoor agricultural navigation historically relied on Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS) augmented by Real-Time Kinematic (RTK) corrections. However, satellite positioning has limitations in the field: thick crop canopies cause multi-path reflections and signal obstruction~\cite{bai2023vision,navone2025gpsfree}. Greenhouse structures also obstruct satellite reception. Furthermore, a GNSS system with RTK corrections is costly~\cite{navone2025gpsfree}. Alternative exteroceptive sensors such as 3D LiDAR provide direct spatial measurements, but their high cost limits the large-scale adoption of agricultural robots~\cite{bai2023vision,navone2025gpsfree}. Monocular RGB cameras offer a cost-effective alternative that captures detailed chromatic and texture cues directly from the scene.

Early vision-based guidance pipelines extracted navigation lines through handcrafted image processing techniques. These approaches compute color vegetation indices to isolate plant matter from soil, apply intensity thresholding, and detect corridor edges using line-fitting algorithms or Hough transforms~\cite{bai2023vision,mendez2023vineyards}. Although computationally lightweight, these calibrated pipelines degrade when ambient field conditions shift. Variable sunlight angles, cast shadows from foliage, soil moisture gradients, specular reflections on wet leaves, and weed infestations frequently disrupt fixed thresholding rules and generate false boundary estimates.

Deep learning methods, in particular convolutional neural networks for semantic segmentation, overcome some of these limitations. They can detect crop rows despite variations in light levels, occlusions due to weeds, and variations in the background~\cite{silva2023rowdetection}.

However, deep neural networks introduce a practical problem: supervised training requires extensive pixel-level ground truth. Manually labeling agricultural scenes is labor-intensive and must cover multiple crop types, growth stages, and ambient conditions. In addition, running neural networks on compact onboard computers requires balancing segmentation accuracy against processing speed and power consumption, as steering commands must be updated fast enough to guide the vehicle in real time.

This thesis addresses these challenges through a modular pipeline that combines automatic dataset annotation with SAM~3, real-time onboard semantic segmentation, and deterministic geometric reference extraction for local robot guidance.

\section{Problem and approach}
\label{sec:problem_approach}

The robot must drive along the path between two crop rows with local guidance from the color images of one camera: it keeps its position relative to the rows visible in the image and does not build or use a map of the field.

Figure~\ref{fig:complete_pipeline} in Chapter~\ref{cap:platform} shows the steps that run on the robot for each camera frame:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Segmentation.} An LR-ASPP network with a MobileNetV3 backbone takes the camera image and predicts a binary mask of the path between the rows. The masks used to train it were generated by SAM~3 without manual correction. SAM~3 runs only offline, to label the training images, and not on the robot.
\item \textbf{Path extraction.} The path extractor reduces the mask to a straight centerline of the corridor in image coordinates. The predicted mask can contain holes and false path regions, so the extractor also returns a status, \texttt{ok}, \texttt{degraded} or \texttt{lost}. Chapter~\ref{cap:geometric_reference} compares five extractors with the same input and output, and all five were tested in closed loop on the robot.
\item \textbf{Control.} The controller steers the robot to keep it at the center of the path between the rows. It computes the lateral error of the centerline from the image center at a fixed image row, filters it, and converts it into an angular velocity command at constant linear speed. If no valid centerline is found for more than 0.5~s, the robot stops.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Objectives of the thesis}
\label{sec:objectives}

The main objectives of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
\item Evaluate whether manual annotation can be replaced by labels generated automatically by SAM~3 from text prompts, without manual correction, by comparing networks trained on SAM~3 labels with a network trained on manual masks.
\item Select a segmentation architecture balancing accuracy and inference speed for robotic deployment, through offline evaluation of FCN, DeepLabV3, and LR-ASPP.
\item Evaluate the effect of output label configurations (four-class, three-class, and binary path) on path segmentation accuracy.
\item Develop extractors that convert segmentation masks into local image-space navigation references: a geometric one that fits the two corridor edges (Strip-IRLS) and a first version of an edge regression network (EdgeNet). Compare them with three baseline algorithms.
\item Integrate the selected perception and geometric components on a robot platform for field validation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Organization of the work}
\label{sec:organization}

The thesis is organized as follows:

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{cap:stato_arte}} reviews vision-based methods for agricultural row navigation, covering classical and learning-based segmentation approaches, geometric extraction techniques, and experimental validation in related work.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{cap:architectures}} presents the segmentation networks evaluated to choose the one used on the robot, FCN, DeepLabV3 and LR-ASPP with a MobileNetV3 backbone, and SAM~3, which generates the training labels.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{cap:segmentation}} describes dataset preparation with manual annotation and automatic SAM~3 labeling, empirical comparisons among the three architectures on lettuce, and evaluations of supervision strategies across label configurations.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{cap:geometric_reference}} presents the two extractors developed in this work, which convert corridor masks into navigation references: Strip-IRLS, based on horizontal-strip interval selection and independent edge fitting, and EdgeNet, a network that regresses the corridor edges, still at an early stage. Three baseline algorithms are described for comparison.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{cap:platform}} describes the robot and its camera, the complete pipeline that runs on the robot from the camera image to the steering command, its integration in ROS, the timing on the onboard computer, and the Processor-in-the-Loop test.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{cap:experiments}} compares the five extractors offline, on synthetic masks and on manually annotated frames, and reports the closed-loop tests of the robot in the field, with the trajectory measured by a total station.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{cap:conclusions}} summarizes the main findings, discusses limitations, and identifies directions for future work.
